# Code-gated modules beat one giant prompt

The best-evidenced way to rebuild the 800-line patient-coordinator prompt is to let code, not the model, decide which rules load and which rule wins, and to confine the model to drafting a reply that deterministic validators can check. Concretely: split the prompt into roughly 8–12 modules tagged by product line, conversation stage and intent; route each SMS with a regex table first and a Haiku JSON classifier as fallback; assemble a byte-stable shared core (cached) plus the matching modules in a fixed order; resolve conflicts with a forbid-overrides-permit rule registry in TypeScript that logs the determining rule; check every URL and price against this turn's tool results and retry once with the allowed values named; make escalation a persisted conversation state whose handoff sentence is a code constant; and record, per reply, the manifest of loaded module IDs plus each named validator's outcome. The reason to decompose is quality, not cost: a 25k-token prompt costs about **$0.0025 per message on Haiku 5.5**, but claude-3.5-haiku's instruction-following fell from **98% at 10 instructions to 43% at 100** ([IFScale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538)), and accuracy dropped from **0.92 to 0.68** as prompts grew from ~250 to ~3,000 tokens with the task held constant ([Levy et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14848)). The sharpest contradiction to the initial plan is that priority numbers inside the prompt do not work on a Haiku-class model: Claude-3 Haiku resolved conflicting instructions correctly **27.9%** of the time on IHEval and a priority sentence did not help even GPT-4o ([IHEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08745v2)), whereas moving policy into a code engine raised τ²-bench compliance from **48% to 93%** ([PCAS](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16708v2)). On a 65-case suite a single run cannot distinguish differences under roughly 14 points; three repetitions with paired per-case scoring bring that to about 7 ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1)). Model-picked skill files, sub-agents on the reply path, DSPy/GEPA and Haiku-class LLM judges are each either unmeasured, counter-indicated for a sequential single-reply task, or worth trying only after the eval harness exists.

## A 25k-token prompt costs cents but drops half its rules

Start with the arithmetic that reframes the project. At list price, Haiku 5.5 charges **$0.10 per million input tokens** and Haiku 4.5 **$1**, so a 25k-token system prompt costs about $0.0025 per message uncached on Haiku 5.5 (≈$0.00025 with a cache hit) and about $0.025 (≈$0.0025 cached) on Haiku 4.5 ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). A 65-message suite therefore costs cents whichever way it is built, and problem 5 on the owners' list ("every message pays full prompt cost") is real but not the reason to spend ten hours. The reason is what the length does to a small model's ability to follow rules.

Four independent lines of evidence converge. Position effects: in multi-document QA, GPT-3.5-Turbo's accuracy drops by **more than 20 points** when the relevant document sits mid-context and in the worst case falls below its **56.1% closed-book** baseline ([Liu et al., "Lost in the Middle"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)). Pure length: with the task fixed and only padding varied, accuracy fell from **0.92 at ~250 tokens to 0.68 at ~3,000**, with degradation visible past 500 tokens and chain-of-thought failing to mitigate it in most models ([Levy et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14848)). Distractors: across 18 models and 194,480 calls, focused ~300-token prompts beat full ~113k-token prompts on every model, Claude's gap was the largest and was driven by abstentions, and **even one distractor reduced accuracy** while four compounded the drop ([Chroma, "Context Rot"](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot)). Instruction count: on IFScale, claude-3.5-haiku scored **98.0 / 78.0 / 43.4 / 16.6 / 8.5%** at 10 / 50 / 100 / 250 / 500 instructions ("exponential decay"), while gpt-4.1 held 95.4% at 100 and gemini-2.5-pro 98.4%; models show a primacy bias in which later instructions are violated more, peaking at 150–200 instructions, and omission is the dominant error ([Jaroslawicz et al., IFScale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538)). ManyIFEval finds consistent degradation across 10 LLMs as instructions rise from 1 to 10 ([Harada et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051)), SCALEDIF attributes much of the decline to conflicts that multiply as rules accumulate ([SCALEDIF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14842v1)), and appending generic rules dropped Qwen 2.5's RAG score from **26/30 to 9/30** ([Commey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22025)). None of these studies used Haiku 4.5 or 5.5, an SMS task, or policy-style rules, so the absolute numbers do not transfer; the direction and the small-model sensitivity do. A per-route bundle of 10–30 rules sits in the range where claude-3.5-haiku was still at or above 78%.

The production measurements that most resemble this rebuild show that fixed context can be cut by roughly two-thirds without quality loss, and that removing confusable material improves quality rather than merely saving money. LangChain's Deep Agents v0.7 removed its base system prompt, trimmed tool descriptions 43% and made the planning tool opt-in, cutting base input tokens **65% (~6k → ~2k)** with reward "held steady" ([LangChain](https://langchain.com/blog/deep-agents-v0-7)). Anthropic's tool search cut pre-work context from ~77K to ~8.7K tokens (**−85%**) and raised MCP-eval accuracy for Opus 4 from **49% to 74%** ([Anthropic, "Advanced tool use"](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/advanced-tool-use)). A quantized Llama 3.1 8B failed a benchmark with 46 tools and passed with 19, and dynamic tool selection improved BFCL results by 44% ([Breunig](https://www.dbreunig.com/2025/06/26/how-to-fix-your-context.html)). The Policy Compiler moved the policy document out of the prompt into an engine, cutting tokens only **3.3%** but raising τ²-bench compliance from 48% to 93% ([PCAS](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16708v2)). The owners' problem 1 ("attention wasted") has direct empirical support: more candidate instructions, worse selection.

Caching discipline decides whether the modular design actually lowers per-message cost or raises it. Anthropic caches by exact prefix in the order tools → system → messages, cache reads cost **0.1×** base input, 5-minute writes 1.25× and 1-hour writes 2×, up to four breakpoints are free, and any change to tool definitions invalidates the tools, system and messages caches at once ([Anthropic prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). Two traps matter here. The minimum cacheable prompt is **512 tokens on Haiku 5.5 but 4,096 on Haiku 4.5**, and shorter prompts are silently not cached, so aggressive pruning on Haiku 4.5 can push the shared core below the threshold ([Anthropic prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). And the default 5-minute TTL will lapse between patient replies on a bursty SMS channel, so the suite run back-to-back will overstate the real hit rate. Manus calls the KV-cache hit rate "the single most important metric for a production-stage AI agent," reports a ~100:1 input:output ratio, and advises keeping the prefix stable, context append-only and serialization deterministic ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)). A secondary field report describes a 4,000-token prefix running at a **7% hit rate** because one dynamic identifier sat mid-prompt; moving it to the end raised hits to 74% and cut monthly cost 59% ([DigitalOcean](https://www.digitalocean.com/community/tutorials/prompt-caching-in-practice-hit-rate), body not retrievable). The request layout that follows is: all 14 tool definitions, byte-identical and in the same order every call; the shared core (identity, tone, escalation triggers, payment-security rules) with a `cache_control` breakpoint; then the per-message modules and the conversation. No timestamps or patient data above the breakpoint, and no per-route tool subsets.

## Code should pick the module; the model only breaks ties

Anthropic's Agent Skills are the obvious template for "load only what a message needs," and the mechanism is worth stating precisely: at startup only each skill's `name` and `description` are pre-loaded into the system prompt, Claude reads the full SKILL.md when it judges the skill relevant, and bundled files load only if the skill points to them, so bundled context is "effectively unbounded" ([Anthropic, "Equipping agents for the real world with Agent Skills"](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills)). The authoring limits give a concrete spec: name ≤ 64 characters, description ≤ 1,024 characters, body under 500 lines, descriptions written in third person because "inconsistent point-of-view can cause discovery problems," and content organized by domain so Claude "only needs to read sales-related schemas, not finance or marketing data" ([Skill authoring best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/agent-skills/best-practices)). Two facts should temper enthusiasm. Anthropic reports **no token-savings or accuracy numbers** for skills at all; the only numbers are limits ([Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills)). And once a skill loads "its content stays in context across turns, so every line is a recurring token cost" ([Claude Code skills](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/skills)). A practitioner audit of Anthropic's own skills adds that "Claude under-triggers skills far more than it over-triggers them" and that descriptions need to be "a little pushy" ([web-developpeur.com](https://www.web-developpeur.com/en/blog/skills-claude-code-patterns-officiels)). Model-picked loading is flexible, needs no state machinery, and is the right fallback for ambiguous messages, but it is unmeasured, under-triggers, and can only be attributed if the load is a logged tool call rather than a silent file read.

The coding tools that solved the same problem at scale all combine a small always-on file with code-scoped files and explicit precedence by order. Cursor loads a rule automatically when a glob matches, pulls it in by description only when the agent judges it relevant, and caps rules at 500 lines ([Cursor rules](https://cursor.com/docs/context/rules)). Claude Code loads `.claude/rules/*.md` files with `paths:` frontmatter "only when Claude works with matching files," targets "under 200 lines per CLAUDE.md file" because "longer files consume more context and reduce adherence," and states plainly that instruction files are "context, not enforced configuration. To block an action regardless of what Claude decides, use a PreToolUse hook" ([Claude Code memory](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory)). Codex concatenates AGENTS.md files root-down so "files closer to your current directory override earlier guidance," with a 32 KiB default cap and advice to "split instructions across nested directories when you hit the cap" ([Codex AGENTS.md](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/agent-configuration/agents-md)). Translate "file path" into "conversation state, product line, intent" and this is the assembler for an SMS bot: a `policy/` folder where each module carries frontmatter `{ product, stage, intent }`, a TypeScript function that reads the conversation record and concatenates core plus matching modules in a fixed order, and a log line listing which modules were included for each reply. The researchers estimate 2–3 hours for this, and it is deterministic, cacheable and testable in a way model-picked loading is not.

The support-automation vendors run the same split at the procedure level and add two details worth copying. Intercom Fin "checks each customer message against the trigger description" of every Procedure (trigger text limited to 256 characters), recommends "When NOT to use" examples specifically "to prevent false triggers from similar but unrelated queries," treats guidance as additive ("one doesn't cancel out the other"), and keeps default escalation triggers that "always apply" regardless of which procedure is running ([Intercom, "Building Fin Procedures"](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/13449439)). Decagon runs intent extraction → AOP selection → RAG fallback on every message ([Decagon agent architecture](https://decagon.ai/modules/agent-architecture)). Nobody publishes how ties between two matching procedures are broken, which is the gap the next section fills with code.

Three further pieces of guidance shape what goes in the shared core. Anthropic's context-engineering post sets the target as "the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens," recommends starting with "a minimal prompt tested on the best available model, then add instructions and examples based on observed failures," and prefers "a curated set of diverse, canonical cases rather than a long list of edge-case rules" ([Anthropic, "Effective context engineering"](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)). The one team that measured a planning/todo tool dropped it from the default because "evals showed the planning prompt and write_todos tool did not significantly improve performance" ([LangChain](https://langchain.com/blog/deep-agents-v0-7)), so do not build one for an SMS bot. And on tools: Anthropic recommends deferred tool loading when definitions exceed roughly 10K tokens or there are 10+ tools, but calls it "less beneficial with fewer than 10 tools" ([Anthropic, "Advanced tool use"](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/advanced-tool-use)); 14 tools is near that lower bound, so measure the definitions' token count and, if they are under ~10K, keep all 14 loaded on every call. Manus's rule is "mask, don't remove": adding or removing tools mid-task "invalidates the cache and confuses the model when earlier turns reference missing tools," so gate tools by a state machine (new → qualified → quoted → deposit → scheduled) rather than by changing the tool list ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)). The hard money and safety rules (no price before a clinic is chosen, no scheduling before a deposit) belong in that gate, not in prose that competes with other rules.

## Regex first, Haiku fallback, re-route every message

Every routing approach is a documented pattern; the design choice is which stage runs first and what happens at low confidence. The strongest comparative numbers come from a June 2026 preprint that pitted classical classifiers against zero-shot Claude Haiku 4.5. On the broad 150-intent CLINC150 set, Haiku zero-shot was statistically tied with a fully fine-tuned RoBERTa (**88.5% vs 89.1%**, p = 0.24) with no training data, and it dominated on the two things a regex router is worst at: out-of-scope recall (**85.6%** for Haiku vs 58.1% RoBERTa vs 36.4% TF-IDF) and noisy input (92.5% vs 80.0% for TF-IDF at 0 dB) ([Rodrigues & Vas](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20371)). A plain TF-IDF plus logistic-regression baseline still scored 95.2% on ATIS and 80.9% on CLINC150, which is why lexical routing remains competitive on narrow in-domain data ([Rodrigues & Vas](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20371)). The cost of the LLM router is latency: median **981 ms, p95 1,787 ms** over 1,000 calls, "above the 500 ms conversational bar," and about $0.25 per 1K calls at Haiku 4.5 list price ([Rodrigues & Vas](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20371)). Guarded Query Routing reached a similar conclusion in law, finance and healthcare domains: a WideMLP router hit ~88% at under 4 ms, fastText ~80% at under 1 ms, and LLM routers ~91% at 62 ms local or 669 ms remote, with the authors noting their findings "challenge the automatic reliance on LLMs for (guarded) query routing" ([Šléher et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14524)). For a seven-ish route space (pricing, clinics, packages, payment, photos, scheduling, other/handoff) the Haiku classifier is operating far below the 150-label regime where it tied RoBERTa; the risk is the extra second, not accuracy.

Embedding routers do not earn their dependency here. The Aurelio semantic-router project publishes no accuracy or latency benchmarks ([GitHub README](https://github.com/aurelio-labs/semantic-router)); the one controlled test of sentence-embedding kNN found **75.1%** on CLINC150 with full training data and only **64.2%** with five examples per intent, below TF-IDF ([Rodrigues & Vas](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20371)); and a July 2026 audit of four open-source routers found an "Always-Mid" baseline matched the Aurelio router exactly on three of four benchmarks and within 0.003 on the fourth, with three of the four producing constant or near-constant assignments ([Kumar & Saminathan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14641)). Anthropic offers no embeddings API, so this would also be a new vendor. Likewise, RouteLLM's headline (95% of GPT-4 quality at 26% GPT-4 calls) and FrugalGPT's (up to 98% cost reduction) are measured against a GPT-4-class default and require preference training data ([LMSYS](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/); [FrugalGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176)); with Haiku already the floor, the only sensible cascade runs upward (Haiku → Sonnet → human on low confidence) as a quality step, and a Haiku 5.5 → Sonnet 5 escalation is a 20× input-price step that still totals cents over the suite ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)).

The hybrid "cheap stage first, LLM for the uncertain residue" is the measured pattern. Arora et al. report a SetFit-plus-LLM uncertainty router that stays "within 2% of native LLM accuracy with 50% less latency" ([Arora et al., EMNLP 2024 Industry](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01627)), and Zaera et al. deploy an in-scope classifier whose high-uncertainty cases alone go to an LLM, "handling tens of thousands of user interactions daily" ([Zaera et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01541)). No controlled study compares hand-written regex to an LLM classifier on paraphrases, so the regex-first ordering rests on vendor and practitioner material rather than a benchmark; on 65 messages every regex miss can be read by hand, which is the practical justification. Kore.ai's route-design rules are the right constraints: "make route descriptions mutually exclusive," because "if two routes could match the same request, customers may experience inconsistent routing," and always "add a route for human help, unsupported requests, or unclear requests" ([Kore.ai](https://docs.kore.ai/agent-platform/abl/howto/design-supervisor-routing-agent)). Zendesk's new tooling that flags overlapping or duplicate intents is indirect evidence that non-overlapping route design is where production teams spend their effort ([Zendesk](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/10198478867994-Announcing-intent-quality-recommendations-in-intelligent-triage)).

Misroute recovery in production is structural rather than clever. Decagon re-runs intent extraction "with each new message," Intercom Fin asks the customer to clarify when output "doesn't meet the engine's certainty parameters" and escalates when safety checks fail ([Decagon](https://decagon.ai/modules/agent-architecture); [Intercom Fin engine](https://intercom.help/intercom/en/articles/9929230-the-fin-ai-engine)), and practitioner guidance converges on confidence bands with an explicit escalation tier ([tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/04/16/intent-classification-agent-routers)). Threshold values in the sources (0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.85) are examples, not validated defaults. The cheapest version for this build: `route(message) = regexRoutes(message) ?? haikuClassify(message)`, with zero or more than two regex hits also sent to Haiku; a forced tool-use schema `{routes: string[], confidence: "high" | "low", out_of_scope: boolean}` with the classifier prefix cached; re-route on every message so a wrong route lasts one turn; the shared handoff and safety module loaded regardless of route; the "unclear" label mapped to a general bundle whose only write tool is "hand off"; and a log line `{stage, routes, confidence, modulesLoaded}` per turn. The suite itself becomes the misroute detector by asserting the expected module set per message and flagging any turn where regex and Haiku disagree.

## Priority numbers fail on Haiku; forbid-beats-permit in code works

The initial plan put precedence numbers inside the prompt. The evidence says that is the weakest option available. IHEval, a 3,538-example benchmark that scores instruction-hierarchy behavior programmatically, found average accuracy collapsing when instructions conflict: GPT-4o **91.0 → 70.0**, GPT-4o mini 84.2 → 44.3, Claude-3 Sonnet 85.1 → 30.7, **Claude-3 Haiku 75.9 → 27.9**, Llama-3.1 8B 55.6 → 11.3 ([IHEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08745v2)). Adding an instruction-priority sentence to the system prompt did not reliably help (GPT-4o 70.0 → 67.2; Mistral-Large 29.4 → 28.3), and the authors conclude dedicated training rather than prompt engineering is needed ([IHEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08745v2)). OpenAI's instruction-hierarchy paper reached the same place from the other side: its fine-tuned model improved system-prompt-extraction defense by 63% and held-out jailbreak robustness by over 30%, but a prompting-only baseline that described the hierarchy "performed much worse," and the only place numeric priority levels appear (system = 0, user = 10, images = 20, tool text = 30) is in that weak baseline ([Wallace et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13208)). With no fine-tuning available and a Haiku-class model, conflict resolution has to happen in code before the prompt is assembled.

What the policy documents and policy engines use instead is a small ordered list of sources plus one combining rule, never a number per rule. OpenAI's Model Spec orders root > system > developer > user > guideline > no authority, treats a lower-level instruction that conflicts with a higher one as inapplicable, gives tool outputs and quoted text no instruction authority by default, and defaults to inaction when two root principles conflict ([OpenAI Model Spec](https://model-spec.openai.com/2026-08-18.html)). Anthropic's constitution orders broad safety > broad ethics > guideline compliance > genuine helpfulness, calls that prioritization "holistic rather than strict," places hard constraints outside the weighing entirely, and treats a conflict between guidelines and ethics as "a flaw to be fixed" rather than resolved case by case ([Anthropic constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution)). The reusable structure is a three-tier split: hard constraints that are never weighed, a short ordered list of rule categories, and a source order. The monolith's "HARD RULE: this overrides X on this turn" sections conflate all three.

Authorization engines show how to make "rule X overrides rule Y" testable. Cedar's semantics are "if any `forbid` policy evaluates to true, then the final result is Deny," otherwise Allow if a `permit` is satisfied, otherwise Deny, with no priority numbers and no ordering; the response's diagnostics list the "determining policies" ([Cedar docs](https://docs.cedarpolicy.com/auth/authorization.html)). The 2026 Policy Compiler (PCAS) uses the same allow-unless-denied structure over a Datalog-derived language, returns a decision trace, annotates denials with `@deny_message` and `@suggestion` so the agent can "retry with a corrected request," and on τ²-bench raised average compliance from **48% to 93%** with zero policy violations at about 20% more runtime ([PCAS](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16708v2)). APPA records every ruling and dispatch atomically to an append-only log and cut attack success from 31–50% to 0–7% ([APPA](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.24625v1)); a policy-as-logic approach reports roughly 10× lower token usage than policy-as-prompt ([Policy-as-logic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11905)). None of these are TypeScript or confirmed open source, so they are design references; a flat registry of predicates suffices.

Translated into the build, each rule becomes a TypeScript object `{ id, kind: 'forbid' | 'require' | 'permit', appliesWhen(ctx), promptFragment?, validator? }`; an `evaluate(ctx)` function returns the active rules and the determining ones; only active rules' fragments enter the prompt; and one JSON log line per turn records `{ ruleId, kind, fired, determining }`. "X overrides Y" is then a unit test: construct a context where both apply and assert the winner and the determining-rule list. Source order is handled by concatenation order, with shared modules first and product-specific modules after, matching the "nearer file overrides" rule of Codex and Cursor ([Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/agent-configuration/agents-md); [Cursor](https://cursor.com/docs/context/rules)). Within a module, order still matters: IFScale's primacy bias means instructions near the end of a block are the most likely to be dropped ([IFScale](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.11538)), so put each module's non-negotiables first. Claude Code's own warning closes the case for doing this in code: "if two instructions contradict each other, Claude may pick one arbitrarily," and its remedy is an audit command plus an `InstructionsLoaded` hook that logs "which CLAUDE.md and rules files are loaded, when they load, and why" ([Claude Code memory](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory)), which is exactly the owners' problem 7 answered by logging rather than by prose.

## Validate deterministically, retry once, name the allowed values

"Deterministic validators plus one retry with the violation fed back" is a known, effective pattern: it is the default of Guardrails AI (REASK with `num_reasks` = 1, the reask prompt auto-including the failed criteria) ([Guardrails AI](https://www.guardrailsai.com/docs/api_reference_markdown/guards)), PydanticAI (`ModelRetry` with an output retry budget of 1) ([PydanticAI](https://pydantic.dev/docs/ai/core-concepts/output/)), Instructor (`max_retries=N` meaning N+1 calls) ([Instructor](https://python.useinstructor.com/concepts/retrying/)), and the enforcement loop of PCAS. The research explains why it works and the initial plan's version of it would not. Self-correction without external feedback hurts: GPT-4 on GSM8K went **95.5 → 91.5 → 89.0** and GPT-3.5 on CommonSenseQA 75.8 → 38.1 → 41.8 under intrinsic self-critique, while oracle feedback lifted GPT-4's HotpotQA from 49.0 to 59.0 ([Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.01798)); a TACL survey finds no prior success from prompted self-feedback outside tasks "exceptionally suited" to it and concludes "self-correction works well in tasks that can use reliable external feedback" ([Kamoi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297)). A deterministic validator is precisely that reliable external feedback.

Two details of the retry message carry most of the value. VeriHarness ran paired TextWorld games under a four-call cap and found that feedback naming the failure location, the observed value and the "admissible alternatives" raised success from **14/50 to 36/50** (Qwen2.5-Coder-14B) and 8/50 to 29/50 (Llama-3.1-8B), while location plus value alone performed close to raw diagnostics, so most of the gain comes from listing what is allowed ([VeriHarness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14167)). Self-Correction Bench found a **64.5%** "blind spot" across 14 models: they fix an error presented as external but often fail on the identical error attributed to themselves ([Self-Correction Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02778)). So the retry should read "A validator rejected the previous reply. Violations: […]. Allowed URLs this turn: […]. Allowed prices: […]. Rewrite the reply," not "review your answer." How many retries: most of the gain lands on the first. Self-correcting vision agents went from ≈62% to ≈70% on the first retry and saturated near 79% by attempt five ([arXiv 2601.11637](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.11637)); Reflexion reports "an immediate spike between the first two trials" ([Reflexion](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.11366)); Prompt2Craft found no significant gain past two rounds ([arXiv 2512.04568](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.04568)); and one code-repair study measured "retry confusion" in 3/50 (**6%**) of Sonnet items with the net effect still positive ([arXiv 2609.28488](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28488)). Cap the loop at one retry, send a safe fallback or the escalation sentence on a second failure, and log `{turn, validatorIds, retried, retryFixed}` so first-retry fix rate per validator becomes a dashboard number; practitioners add that if the loop fires on more than 15% of requests the prompt or schema is the bug ([TianPan](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-09-structured-output-failures-production-llm)). No peer-reviewed first-retry fix rate exists for chat replies validated by deterministic checks; expect to measure it.

API-enforced structured output handles shape, not truth, and its limits decide which of the four checks it can absorb. Anthropic's constrained decoding guarantees valid JSON, required keys, enums, a regex subset and string formats including `uri`, but returns a 400 for `minimum`/`maximum`, `minLength`/`maxLength` and recursive schemas; refusals and `max_tokens` truncation bypass the schema; the first use of a schema compiles a grammar (cached 24 hours); changing `output_config.format` invalidates the prompt cache; and citations cannot be combined with structured outputs ([Anthropic structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs); [Anthropic citations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/citations)). A thin schema `{ reply: string, url: string | null, escalate: boolean, reason?: enum }` therefore makes "URL on the last line" structural: code renders reply plus newline plus URL, and the model never obeys a formatting rule for it. "No markdown" can be partially expressed as a positive `pattern`, but sentence count and URL/price provenance need post-generation checks on either API, and refusal/`max_tokens` are a separate branch (plain retry with a higher limit, or fallback) rather than a violation retry. Keep one stable schema per reply type so the grammar compile and cache invalidation happen once.

The provenance check is stricter and simpler than anything benchmarked. Published attribution checkers split into deterministic link checks that are near-perfect and claim-support checks that are not: across 14 LLMs on research queries, automatic link validity was **94–100%** for frontier models while LLM-judged fact support ranged 24.4–76.8% and fell from 78.6% to 16.7% for GPT-5.4 as tool calls rose from 2 to 150 ([Cited but Not Verified](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06635v1)); in medicine, GPT-4 with RAG left about 30% of statements unsupported and a GPT-4 verifier agreed with physicians 88% of the time ([Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02008)); Vectara's HHEM detector reaches 82.2% accuracy with no published false-positive rate ([HHEM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22416v1)). None of that is accurate enough to gate a price in an SMS. The rule here is set membership: extract every URL (regex) and every price (currency/number regex with a normalizer for thousands separators, locale formats and ranges) from the reply, build the allowed sets from this turn's tool results, and fail on any token outside the set; the expected false-positive rate is whatever the extractor mis-tokenizes, so write fixtures for "$1,500–$2,000" and "1.500 €" and decide a rule for computed totals ("two sessions = $3,000"). The guardrail frameworks are a reference API, not a dependency: NeMo adds three API calls and **+65% tokens** per request for its LLM-based output rails ([Liu, "Quantifying the Safety Tax"](https://zihaoliu.substack.com/p/quantifying-the-safety-tax-where)), Guardrails AI blocked 6/241 benign prompts (2.5%) ([Liu](https://zihaoliu.substack.com/p/quantifying-the-safety-tax-where)), LLM Guard and Rebuff are archived ([protectai/rebuff](https://github.com/protectai/rebuff)), and all are Python. Copy the contract (a validator returns `{ id, pass, violation, allowed? }`; one reask; log the outcome) in TypeScript.

## Escalation is a state transition that code owns

Every product examined (Intercom Fin, Zendesk, Decagon, Forethought, Ada) and the τ-bench family use a hybrid in which deterministic triggers own the explicit and high-stakes cases and the mechanics of the handoff, and a model decides only the judgment cases against a written policy. Intercom's data-driven Escalation Rules fire before Fin answers ("when a rule matches, Fin doesn't answer the question and hands off instead"), a clear request for a human "leads to immediate escalation," and even its machine-learned decider carries hard-coded guardrails ("no offering escalation twice in a row, and no escalating on the first user message unless a guideline explicitly allows it") ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations); [fin.ai research](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/)). Zendesk implements handoff as a "Transfer to agent" flow step and states "the Pass Control API call is always in charge to hand off control" ([Zendesk](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/9755691894298)). τ-bench makes escalation a tool, `transfer_to_human_agents(summary)`, returning the literal "Transfer successful," governed by the policy "transfer the user to a human agent if and only if the request cannot be handled within the scope of your actions" ([τ-bench policy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/wiki.md); [tool source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/tools/transfer_to_human_agents.py)); τ²'s telecom policy orders "first make a tool call to transfer_to_human_agents, and then send the message" with a verbatim fixed sentence ([τ²-bench policy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/telecom/main_policy.md)). The anti-"pretend escalation" pattern is thus structural: the customer-facing sentence exists only after a recorded action.

Nobody uses raw model confidence as the primary gate, and the two sources that measured it explain why. Verbalized confidence is overconfident, with white-box methods only narrowly better at AUROC **0.522–0.605** ([Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063)); max-softmax and entropy baselines "tend to over-escalate" in a hybrid human-AI setting, where a trained meta-model cut expected cost 22–71% relative to them with the optimal threshold near 0.5 on a validation sweep ([Bachar et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07006v1)). A practitioner protocol ranks task-level signals (contradictory tool data, missing required fields, financial thresholds) as most reliable, calls letting the model judge its own failures "a structurally bad design," and cites post-execution agents predicting 73% task success against 35% actual ([tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-10-escalation-protocol-agent-to-human-handoffs), secondary citation for the last figure). The only prompt-only escalation classifier with published numbers, Gusto's, landed at about **90% precision and 90% recall** before any training; its BERT successor reached ~94%/~93% on ~3,500 labeled turns with the threshold tuned to recall ≥ 0.90 ([Gusto via daily.dev](https://daily.dev/posts/from-prompt-to-classifier-a-production-case-study-6p2dazqlv), secondary; original returned 403). Intercom's trained ModernBERT decider reports 97.4% escalation accuracy and 98.7% guideline-citation AUC but no precision or recall ([fin.ai research](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/)). A 90/90 ceiling on the judgment class, with near-100% recall by construction on the hard-trigger class, is the realistic target for a ten-hour build.

Prompt-only escalation rules will fire inconsistently, which is the deeper reason the hard triggers belong in code. τ-bench's pass^k metric shows the same model following a policy in one run and not another: gpt-4o scored **61.2% pass^1** on retail but **under 25% pass^8**, and removing the policy from the prompt dropped airline pass^1 from 33.2 to 10.8 ([τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1)); Sierra's summary is that "function-calling agents were weak at following the rules in policy documents" ([Sierra](https://sierra.ai/blog/benchmarking-ai-agents)). Claude-3-haiku managed 19.0% retail / 14.4% airline pass^1 on the same benchmark ([τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1)). So: a `hardTriggers(message, toolPlan)` function in code for the explicit human-request phrase list and the three named unsupported actions (card charge, moving paid funds, asking a clinic to hold a date), each mapped to a `reason` enum that code validates against the 14-tool set; a `request_human` tool or `escalate` field as the model's only path for residual judgment; the "insist after refusal" rule from τ² airline task 13 (if the bot has already said "I can't do X" and the patient repeats, escalate rather than refuse twice) ([τ² tasks](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/airline/tasks.json)); tool-call failure as a secondary trigger with its own reason code so it can be measured separately. Write the out-of-scope rule narrowly. Intercom warns that "broad guidance increases escalations and lowers resolution rates" and recommends guidance for "the narrowest possible situations" ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations)), and the abstention survey warns that self-check instructions make models "overly cautious with benign inputs" ([Wen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3)); a blanket "when unsure, hand off" is the LLM equivalent of safety over-tuning.

Stopping is the part the initial plan's "escalation flag" leaves out. Intercom users report that after a teammate takes over, "as soon as the customer responds again, Fin jumps back in and interrupts" ([Intercom community](https://community.intercom.com/workflows-7/fin-ai-agent-issues-on-the-whatsapp-channel-12977)), and a 2026 thread describes Fin replying after a teammate answered ([Intercom community](https://community.intercom.com/train-fin-94/fin-replying-to-team-member-15067)). The stop condition must be a persisted `conversation.state = 'HANDED_OFF'` checked at the top of the inbound handler, with a human "release" action returning it to the bot, and a code-owned auto-acknowledgement for patients who text again during off hours. The handoff sentence is a code constant sent only after the escalation record is persisted; a cheap eval guard is a regex on outbound text for "connect you / transferred / a colleague will" whenever `escalate=false`. The human should receive a validated payload, not a free-text summary: Decagon's handoff schema carries objective and state, evidence with provenance, attempts and exclusions, and a schema version, and warns that "free-text summaries drop provenance" ([Decagon](https://decagon.ai/glossary/what-is-an-ai-agent-handoff-schema)); a minimal `{ reason, patientGoal, toolCallsMade[], unresolvedQuestion, transcriptRef, traceId }` is free because the system already has those fields.

Measure escalation with the abstention confusion matrix: abstention precision N5/(N3+N5), recall N5/(N2+N4+N5), and over-abstention on safe prompts N3/(N1+N2+N3), where N3 is the false handoff that wastes a coordinator's time and N4 the missed handoff that costs trust ([Wen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3)). Label the test set by whether a human should have been involved and report the operational escalation rate alongside, because the industry's definition of "handoff" is purely operational (Zendesk counts a messaging resolution only after 2 hours idle, up to 72, and excludes any escalated conversation; Ada marks escalated conversations "Not Resolved immediately after the handoff") ([Zendesk](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/5352026794010); [Ada](https://docs.ada.cx/docs/generative/measure-success/understand-and-improve-your-ai-agent-s-automated-resolution-rate)). Benchmarks for the rate are vendor estimates: Decagon calls under 20% healthy ([Decagon](https://decagon.ai/glossary/what-is-escalation-rate)), and one ecommerce vendor suggests 12–18% for simple categories, 30–40% for regulated ones and "about half" for medical devices ([Alhena](https://alhena.ai/blog/ai-escalation-rate-optimization-ecommerce/)), so a 30%+ rate for a medical-adjacent flow is not by itself a defect. Run each hard-trigger case 3–5 times and require every run to agree, reporting pass^k next to precision and recall. Finally, disclosure: EU AI Act Article 50(1) requires people be told they are interacting with an AI from the first interaction and applies from **2 August 2026** ([artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)); Utah SB 226, effective 2025-05-07, requires prominent disclosure for high-risk generative-AI interactions including those collecting health information, with a safe harbor when the AI "clearly and conspicuously discloses it is nonhuman … at the outset of the interaction and throughout" ([Utah SB 226](https://le.utah.gov/Session/2025/bills/enrolled/SB0226.pdf)); California AB 3030 adds a disclaimer and a route to a human provider for AI-generated clinical communications from covered providers ([Petrie-Flom Center](http://petrieflom.law.harvard.edu/2024/10/17/health-care-ai-and-the-law-an-emerging-regulatory-landscape-in-california/)). The cheapest compliance is an AI self-identification in the first SMS and a standing "reply HUMAN to reach a coordinator" line; applicability of AB 3030 is a question for counsel.

## The trace is a manifest plus named validators; sub-agents become one worker call

No tracing standard knows what a "rule section" is. The OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions (still Development status) define inference spans (`chat {model}`), tool spans (`execute_tool {tool}`) and agent spans (`invoke_agent {agent}`), with `gen_ai.conversation.id`, token usage, finish reasons and tool call IDs, and with `gen_ai.system_instructions` and message content as opt-in attributes because they are "likely to contain sensitive information"; there is no attribute for prompt version, prompt section or "instructions loaded" ([OTel GenAI spans v1.37.0](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions/v1.37.0/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-spans.md)). OpenInference adds `llm.prompt_template.version` and the `PROMPT` and `GUARDRAIL` span kinds, which fit prompt assembly and validators ([OpenInference spec](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Arize-ai/openinference/main/spec/semantic_conventions.md)). Langfuse links a generation to one prompt name and version via `langfuse.observation.prompt.name/version` OTel attributes and aggregates latency, tokens, cost and scores per version, and only top-level metadata keys are filterable ([Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/docs/prompt-management/features/link-to-traces); [Langfuse OTel](https://langfuse.com/docs/opentelemetry/get-started)); Braintrust auto-attaches prompt identity for prompts loaded through its SDK and recommends indexing `metadata.prompt_version` ([Braintrust](https://www.braintrust.dev/articles/how-to-monitor-prompt-performance-in-production)); LangSmith has commit hashes but no documented automatic trace link ([LangSmith](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/manage-prompts)). Section-level provenance is therefore a custom attribute everywhere: one `invoke_agent` root per inbound SMS carrying `coordinator.rules.loaded = [{id, version, sha256, reason}]`, `coordinator.rules.skipped`, the router's intent and confidence, a `chat` child with the composed prompt's hash and linked prompt version, one `execute_tool` child per tool call, one `GUARDRAIL`-kind child per named validator with `rule_id`, `passed` and `message`, and root attributes `escalation.fired` and `escalation.source` (`validator:R05`, `router:low-confidence`, `tool:error`, `model:asked`). Because the content is patient health data, store messages and system instructions out of band and put only hashes and references on spans, following the OTel guidance to "store content externally and record references on spans" and Anthropic's practice of monitoring "decision patterns and interaction structures without reading individual conversation contents" ([OTel GenAI spans](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions/v1.37.0/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-spans.md); [Anthropic multi-agent](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/built-multi-agent-research-system)).

Attributing a bad sentence to a specific instruction has no published method. ContextCite fits a linear surrogate over random leave-out ablations of context sources at **32×** the cost of generating the response ([ContextCite](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00729)); AttriBoT approximates leave-one-out more than 300× faster but needs cached activations and proxy models unavailable on a hosted API ([AttriBoT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15102)); and no paper attributes output to a system-prompt instruction. Three practical substitutes exist. Named per-rule validators give exact attribution of a failure to a rule at zero marginal LLM cost, which is Anthropic's stated preference ("the best form of feedback is providing clearly defined rules for an output, then explaining which rules failed and why") and the shape of Sierra's and Decagon's inline supervisors ([Anthropic Agent SDK post](https://claude.com/blog/building-agents-with-the-claude-agent-sdk); [Sierra](https://sierra.ai/product/trust-and-reliability)). Black-box section ablation is an offline debug tool: for one bad reply, replay the trace with each of K loaded sections removed and diff the outputs, K calls total. A model self-report of `applied_rule_ids` in the structured output is cheap and filterable but is an explanation the model generates, and such explanations can be unfaithful to what actually drove the answer ([Turpin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388)); use it as a hint, never as proof.

Sub-agents are the wrong tool for the reply itself and the right tool, in degenerate form, for one side task. Anthropic's conditions for multi-agent are heavy parallelization, information exceeding one context window, and many complex tools; it hurts when agents must share context or have many dependencies, and "the task's value must justify the higher token cost," since agents use about **4× chat tokens and multi-agent systems about 15×** ([Anthropic multi-agent](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/built-multi-agent-research-system)). The 90.2% gain over single-agent Opus 4 is on an open-ended research eval and token usage alone explained 80% of the variance ([Anthropic multi-agent](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/built-multi-agent-research-system)). Google's 180-configuration study found centralized coordination improved a parallelizable financial-reasoning task by 80.9% but "every multi-agent variant we tested degraded performance by **39–70%**" on sequential planning, with coordination cost rising disproportionately as tool count grows ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/towards-a-science-of-scaling-agent-systems-when-and-why-agent-systems-work/)); MAST attributes most multi-agent failures to specification and inter-agent misalignment across 1,000+ annotated traces ([MAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)). A single SMS reply (read thread → fetch records → draft → validate) with 14 tools meets both of Google's "hurts" conditions. The vendors confirm the shape: Fin runs a dedicated 14B "Issue Summarizer" and small models for escalation routing, Sierra assembles agents from 15+ models with input and response supervisors plus a deterministic output guard, Decagon runs a real-time supervisor model, and none of them run an autonomous sub-agent loop per reply or publish its latency ([fin.ai](https://fin.ai/cx-models); [Sierra](https://sierra.ai/blog/constellation-of-models); [Decagon](https://decagon.ai/vs/sierra)). Call-log summarization is the one legitimate context-isolation case ("sifting through large amounts of information where most of it won't be useful") ([Anthropic Agent SDK post](https://claude.com/blog/building-agents-with-the-claude-agent-sdk)): implement it as a single Haiku worker call with its own prompt file, zod output schema and `invoke_agent` span, run in parallel with record lookups or precomputed when a log lands and cached by `(patientId, latestLogId)`, so reply-time latency is at most one extra round-trip and a wrong summary is attributable to the summarizer's prompt version rather than the drafter's.

## Sixty-five cases need three reps and pairing to see ten points

The statistics of a small suite set the floor on what the comparison can claim. For binary scores the standard error is sqrt(p(1−p)/n), so **65 cases at an 80% pass rate give SE ≈ 5.0 points** (95% CI ±9.7); two systems run once each and compared unpaired have an SE of the difference near 7 points, so only differences of ~14 points are two-SE significant ([Miller, "Adding Error Bars to Evals"](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1), researchers' arithmetic). Miller's remedies are pairing and resampling: comparing both systems on the same questions with a per-case correlation of 0.5 cuts estimator variance by a third, and resampling each question K times reduces the within-question term, with K = 2/4/6 cutting it by 1/3, 1/2 and 5/9 toward a limit of 2/3; sampling temperature should never be lowered to fake stability ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1)). Applied here, three repetitions with paired per-case scoring bring the detectable difference to roughly 7 points, and adding the ~60 BAD/GOOD-derived cases to reach ~125 shrinks SE by another ~1/√2, which helps more than going past three reps. Because the cases will cluster on shared packages and fixtures, report clustered standard errors; Miller found them over 3× the naive SE on DROP (1.34 vs 0.44) ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1)). Practitioners run 3–10 trials: τ-bench uses at least 3 ([τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045)), Nubank 5 with 95% CIs ([Nubank](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08867v2)), and Anthropic recommends pass^k "for agents where consistency is essential," noting a 75% per-trial rate gives pass^3 of about 42% ([Anthropic, "Demystifying evals"](https://anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). Report, per condition, pass rate ± SE, pass^3 per case, and the paired per-case difference ± paired SE.

Build the cases from three sources and freeze the tool fixtures. CheckList's three test types map directly: minimum functionality tests are the 65 messages, invariance tests re-send each with typos, lowercase or a different patient name and require the same fact set, and directional tests add "I'm in pain" or "I can't afford this" and require the escalation flag to flip; practitioners using CheckList "created twice as many tests and found almost three times as many bugs" ([Ribeiro et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.04118); [UW IDL](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/check-list)). The ~60 BAD/GOOD pairs are the SPADE idea done by hand: each pair is a prompt delta that already names the failure, so convert each into message + fixture + expected facts + escalation flag + the specific assertion the BAD example violated; SPADE itself mined prompt revision history into assertions, cutting assertion count 14% and false failures 21% across nine pipelines ([SPADE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03038)). Policy-derived cases follow τ-bench's recipe of one policy document, a hidden user scenario and exactly one valid outcome under the policy, and are the cheapest way to cover rules the 65 messages never exercise ([τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045)). Anthropic's guidance is to start with "roughly 20–50 simple tasks drawn from real failures," each with a reference solution that passes all graders to prove the task is solvable and the graders are wired ([Anthropic](https://anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). Each case must pin the 14 tool results as recorded JSON; otherwise run-to-run variance in tool outputs confounds the prompt comparison. promptfoo in TypeScript supports `javascript` assertions, `assert-set` groups with thresholds, `defaultTest` for suite-wide checks and `--repeat n` ([promptfoo assertions](https://www.promptfoo.dev/docs/configuration/expected-outputs/); [promptfoo CLI](https://www.promptfoo.dev/docs/usage/command-line/)); Braintrust's `trialCount` runs each input multiple times and groups by input ([Braintrust](https://braintrust.dev/docs/evaluate/advanced-evaluations)). A ~200-line hand-rolled harness is equivalent for custom checks.

The practitioner consensus (Husain, Shankar, Yan) is to do error analysis on ~100 real traces first, annotating the first 30 yourself and grouping notes into a failure taxonomy, to use binary pass/fail with critiques rather than scores, to "favor assertions or other deterministic checks over LLM-as-judge evaluators," and to treat generic metrics as something that "waste[s] time and create[s] false confidence" ([Hamel Husain & Shreya Shankar, AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)). For this project the ~60 flagged BAD replies are the 100 traces; most codes will map to the existing deterministic checks and only leftover codes (tone, warmth, over-apologizing) justify a judge. If a tone judge is added, the numbers say what it must look like. GPT-4 as a pairwise judge agrees with human experts **85%** of the time on MT-Bench excluding ties, about the human–human level of 81%, but weak judges show severe bias: position-swap consistency was 65.0% for GPT-4, 46.2% for GPT-3.5 and **23.8% for Claude-v1**, and a verbosity attack fooled Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 **91.3%** of the time versus 8.7% for GPT-4 ([Zheng et al., MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)); GPT-4-turbo failed to lower scores for perturbed long-form answers more than half the time ([Eugene Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/llm-evaluators/)); and under a weak starter prompt GPT-4.1-mini collapsed to a single class with inter-model kappa of 0.000 in Nubank's support evaluators ([Nubank](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08867v2)). So run the judge on a Sonnet-class model, pairwise (old-prompt reply vs new-system reply for the same message), binary, in both orders with a tie when the orders disagree, never shown the expected label, with the GOOD examples as the rubric anchor, and keep it non-gating until validated against labeled data: Husain's bar is 30–50 Pass and 30–50 Fail examples per split with TPR and TNR reported separately, warning that below 60 examples "confidence intervals are often too wide" ([Hamel Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/); [evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)). The single most valuable deterministic check is the closed-world URL/price extractor, because it is the one a judge is worst at.

## GEPA fits the compute budget but not the data budget

Automatic prompt optimization is cheap to run and risky to trust at this scale. GEPA extends the metric to return a score plus `feedback_text` extracted from traces and keeps candidates on a per-instance Pareto frontier; on GPT-4.1 mini it lifted an aggregate across six tasks from **53.03 (baseline) to 58.67 (MIPROv2) to 65.22 (GEPA)**, on IFBench (instruction following with unseen output constraints) from 47.79 to 52.72, and on PUPA, a rule-following task, from 78.57 to 94.47, with all GPT-4.1-mini experiments costing under $500 and GEPA's own runs $86 ([Agrawal et al., GEPA](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.19457v2)). Its ablation shows the Pareto bookkeeping is the ingredient: best-candidate selection alone gained +6.05 points, beam search +5.11, Pareto +12.44 on Qwen3-8B, which is why a hand-rolled hill-climb will underperform it by construction ([GEPA](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.19457v2)). Budget arithmetic from the DSPy source (light n = 6, minibatch 35, full eval every 5 steps) puts a `light` run at roughly **500 metric calls** with a 30-case valset and ~640 with 65, matching Nubank's "about 500 iterations" ([DSPy gepa.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/stanfordnlp/dspy/main/dspy/teleprompt/gepa/gepa.py); [Nubank](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08867v2)). Each metric call is one bot turn plus the deterministic checker, so a run finishes in well under an hour on Haiku. Compute is not the constraint.

Data is. Simon Willison's run on a Datasette agent with 20 training and 10 held-out questions moved train accuracy from 90 to 95 and held-out from **95 to 85**, grew the prompt from ~2,400 to ~8,800 characters, plateaued after one accepted mutation because nearly every minibatch already scored 100%, and found that two of three apparent baseline failures were metric bugs whose fix alone raised baseline held-out from 81.7 to 95.0; his conclusion was "at this scale, eval quality dominates optimizer quality" ([Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/2/dspy-datasette-agent-prompts/); [report](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/simonw/research/main/dspy-datasette-agent-prompts/README.md)). The regression mechanism (an added rule colliding with an existing one) is exactly the failure mode of an 800-line prompt with 60 contrastive pairs. GEPA's published train sizes were 45–150 ([GEPA](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.19457v2)); with ~125 cases a 40/60 split leaves ~50 for optimization, the small end of that range. The DSPy docs warn that using the trainset as valset makes GEPA "overfit prompts to the provided trainset" ([DSPy GEPA](https://dspy.ai/current/api/optimizers/GEPA/overview/)), and Husain's advice is to "inspect a few disagreements manually before trying automated prompt tuning," which "can sometimes require hundreds or thousands of evaluations" ([evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)). Tooling adds friction: DSPy and gepa are Python, so the TypeScript harness would be called over HTTP or CLI from `gepa.optimize_anything` ([gepa-ai/gepa](https://github.com/gepa-ai/gepa)); the Ax port runs GEPA natively in TypeScript but its MiPRO needs a Python service, per a third-party guide ([Ax guide](https://instagit.com/ax-llm/ax/ax-optimizer-options-mipro-ace-gepa-usage.md)); a hand-rolled TypeScript hill-climb is about two hours and ~2,400 bot calls but has no Pareto bookkeeping. MIPROv2's gains come mostly from bootstrapped few-shot demos ([Opsahl-Ong et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.11695v2)), which conflict with SMS length constraints and the routed decomposition; skip it.

The highest-value target for GEPA in this project is probably not the bot prompt at all. Nubank's KDD'26 paper optimized binary LLM-judge prompts for support conversations with GEPA (`auto=light`, GPT-4.1-mini base, GPT-5.1 reflection, 40/30/30 split, five repeats) and raised held-out evaluator accuracy from **68.88 to 88.89** on one evaluator and 58.07 to 73.01 on another, while lifting inter-model Cohen's kappa "from near-zero to κ > 0.7 for most model pairs" ([Nubank](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08867v2)). If the team adds a tone judge, optimizing that judge's prompt against the 60 human-labeled pairs is the better-evidenced use of GEPA than optimizing the reply prompt. If the reply prompt is optimized anyway, do it after decomposition and per module (which narrows the collision surface), on the dev split only, with a held-out comparison at three reps, and watch for the optimizer learning to satisfy the checker literally (always appending a URL line) rather than improving judgment.

## A second product line is a folder, a route label and an eval set

The multi-product pattern is consistent across support vendors and coding tools: a shared core that applies to everyone, product-specific content tagged to an audience and loaded only for that product, more-specific items overriding shared ones by order, and a router or product field choosing the active set. Intercom's design is the most explicit: "Audiences now control what Fin knows, while Fin identities control how Fin appears," Content, Guidance and Data Connectors "can each be targeted to an Audience," anything untargeted "will be available to 'Everyone' by default," Fin uses "only the active brand's Help Center content and unbranded content," a workspace supports up to 300 brands, and brand matching uses "the brand's internal ID — not its display name," with mismatches silently excluding articles ([Intercom, "Manage multiple brands"](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/9744851-manage-multiple-brands-in-intercom)). Codex puts repo-wide rules in the root file and service-specific rules in nested files that override ([Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/agent-configuration/agents-md)); Cursor combines nested AGENTS.md files "with more specific instructions taking precedence" ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/docs/context/rules)); Anthropic's skill guidance organizes reference files by domain so unrelated domains never load ([best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/agent-skills/best-practices)). Fin adds a design choice worth copying explicitly: workspace-level guidance "does not automatically apply inside a Procedure" and must be selected, and sub-procedures "are local and can't be called by unrelated procedures" ([Intercom Fin Procedures](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/13449439)), so decide per module whether the shared block is injected, and log it.

Two findings make the separation a correctness requirement rather than tidiness. Context Rot's distractor result ("even one distractor reduced performance, and four compounded the drop") means rules for a second product line are distractors for the first whenever they load together ([Chroma](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot)), which is the owners' problem 3 restated as a measured effect. And the schema-swap experiment shows the router side is the easy part: a RoBERTa fine-tuned on one app's intents scored 96.8% on it and exactly 0% on a second app, while a schema-prompted LLM served both at ~94% with no retraining ([Rodrigues & Vas](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20371)), so a second product line is a new set of labels in the Haiku classifier prompt. Anthropic's routing rationale applies directly: customer-service queries are "directed into different downstream processes, prompts, and tools" because "optimizing for one kind of input can hurt performance on other inputs" ([Anthropic, "Building effective agents"](https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents)).

The concrete mapping is `policy/shared/` (identity, tone, escalation triggers, payment-security rules, the "unclear" and handoff routes) plus `policy/hair/` today and `policy/fertility/` later; a `product` field on the conversation set from the inbound number or campaign, or by the router when ambiguous; the assembler loading `*` modules first and product modules after so product-specific rules override by order; and per-product route labels and per-product eval cases. Adding fertility is a new folder, new labels, new fixtures and a new eval set; `policy/hair/` is not edited, and the regression guard is the existing suite run unchanged, which is how Fin ("central simulation library supports regression testing when any Procedure, policy, or knowledge article changes"), Decagon (regression tests that replay historical transcripts, versioning with rollback) and Sierra (version-controlled snapshots, staging-to-production, instant rollback) describe their own change control ([Fin.ai comparison](https://fin.ai/learn/ai-agent-procedures-aops-journeys), vendor-authored). Because all 14 tool definitions stay constant in every request, the second product adds modules after the cache breakpoint only and the shared prefix keeps caching. Three failure modes to design for: a message that mixes products goes to the "unclear" route; a wrong product tag silently excludes modules (Intercom's ID-mismatch failure), so assert the loaded manifest in the eval; and shared modules that quietly assume hair-transplant facts must be found by running the fertility suite against them.

## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the project more than its parts. The monolith's defect is not that it is expensive but that a Haiku-class model cannot hold hundreds of rules in attention or arbitrate their conflicts, and every published fix for that is the same move: take decisions out of the prompt and put them into code that can be logged and unit-tested, leaving the model to draft. The initial plan's load-bearing ideas were all model-side (model-picked skill files, model-set escalation flag, model-read precedence numbers), and those are precisely the pieces with the weakest or most negative evidence. What remains is a small policy engine around a drafting model: code chooses modules, code resolves conflicts, code checks the output, code owns the handoff state, and the trace is simply what code recorded. A second product line then costs a folder, because nothing product-specific lives anywhere else.

The practical implication for ten hours is ordering. Every method here is measured against the suite, and the suite as it stands cannot see a ten-point change in one run, so the harness with frozen fixtures and paired repeated runs is not a nice-to-have but the instrument that makes the other nine hours interpretable. Expect the first honest numbers to look modest: a prompt-only escalation decider near 90/90 on the judgment class, a first-retry fix rate that only the logs will reveal, and a quality delta from decomposition that the literature predicts but has never measured for a support prompt. The results worth publishing in the README are those first measurements, since no source in this research reports them.

## Ranked shortlist: five methods to try first in ten hours

1. **Eval harness with frozen tool fixtures, deterministic checks, three repetitions and paired per-case reporting (~2.5 h).** Build it first because a single run of 65 cases has SE ≈ 5 points and cannot distinguish differences under ~14 points, and every other method below is judged by it ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1)).
2. **Code-scoped module assembly with a regex-first, Haiku-fallback router and a logged manifest (~3 h).** Split the prompt into `policy/<product>/<module>.md` with `{product, stage, intent}` frontmatter, assemble shared core then product modules in a fixed order behind a byte-stable, cached tool-and-core prefix, re-route every message, and write the loaded-module list to the trace ([Claude Code memory](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory); [Rodrigues & Vas](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20371); [Anthropic prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)).
3. **Deterministic validators plus one retry that names the allowed values (~2 h).** Implement URL/price set membership against this turn's tool results, no-markdown, URL-last via a `{reply, url, escalate}` schema rendered by code, and one-sentence-on-escalation, then retry once with "A validator rejected the previous reply … allowed URLs/prices this turn: […]" and log which validator fired and whether the retry fixed it ([VeriHarness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14167); [Anthropic structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)).
4. **Escalation as a code-owned state transition (~1.5 h).** Hard triggers in code for explicit human requests and the three named unsupported actions with a `reason` enum, a `request_human` tool as the model's only path, a constant handoff sentence sent only after the record persists, a `HANDED_OFF` state that stops generation, and precision/recall/over-abstention plus pass^k on the tagged set ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations); [τ²-bench policy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/telecom/main_policy.md); [Wen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3)).
5. **A forbid/require/permit rule registry with state-gated tools for the 3–5 money and safety rules (~1 h).** Encode "no price before clinic chosen" and "no booking before deposit" as code predicates evaluated per turn with the determining rule logged, and gate the corresponding tools by conversation state instead of relying on precedence prose, since a Haiku-class model resolves prompt-level conflicts correctly only ~28% of the time ([IHEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08745v2); [Cedar](https://docs.cedarpolicy.com/auth/authorization.html); [Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)).

Deferred to a second pass, in order: the "offer, then escalate" class for ambiguous phrasing, a Sonnet-class pairwise tone judge validated on 30 Pass + 30 Fail labels, the inline call-log summarizer worker, black-box section ablation as a debug command, and one GEPA `light` run on the dev split (or on the judge prompt) evaluated on held-out with three reps.

## Surprises: where the evidence contradicts the initial plan

The initial plan was "rule router plus skill files plus validators plus escalation flag, with precedence numbers inside the prompt." The following findings cut against it.

1. **Precedence numbers inside the prompt are the one approach with measured negative evidence.** Claude-3 Haiku resolved conflicting instructions correctly 27.9% of the time on IHEval and a priority sentence did not help even GPT-4o (70.0 → 67.2) ([IHEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08745v2)); numeric priority levels appear in the literature only as Wallace et al.'s weak prompting baseline, which "performed much worse" than training ([Wallace et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13208)); no policy engine uses numbers, and the one that moved policy into code went from 48% to 93% compliance ([PCAS](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16708v2)). Use a `kind` field (forbid/require/permit) and concatenation order in code.
2. **"Skill files" in the Anthropic sense are model-picked, unmeasured and sticky.** Anthropic reports no token or accuracy numbers for Skills, a loaded skill "stays in context across turns," Claude "under-triggers skills far more than it over-triggers them," and attribution works only if the load is a logged tool call ([Anthropic engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills); [Claude Code skills](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/skills); [web-developpeur.com](https://www.web-developpeur.com/en/blog/skills-claude-code-patterns-officiels)). Code-scoped modules (the Cursor glob / Claude Code `paths:` / Codex nested-file pattern) should carry the load; a model-invoked `load_policy` tool is only the fallback for the "unclear" route.
3. **An escalation flag the model sets is not an escalation.** Fin "jumps back in and interrupts" after a human takes over ([Intercom community](https://community.intercom.com/workflows-7/fin-ai-agent-issues-on-the-whatsapp-channel-12977)); τ-bench shows prompt-level policy following is inconsistent (gpt-4o 61.2% pass^1 but under 25% pass^8) ([τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1)); production systems make the handoff a persisted state and a code-owned sentence ordered after a recorded tool call ([τ²-bench policy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/telecom/main_policy.md); [Zendesk](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/9755691894298)).
4. **Model confidence should not gate escalation, and "escalate if unsure" makes things worse.** Verbalized confidence is overconfident (AUROC 0.522–0.605) ([Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063)), softmax/entropy baselines over-escalate ([Bachar et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07006v1)), and Intercom states "broad guidance increases escalations and lowers resolution rates" ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations)). Enumerate the triggers narrowly.
5. **Do not change the tool subset per route.** Any tool-definition change invalidates the tools, system and messages caches ([Anthropic prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)), and removing tools mid-task "confuses the model when earlier turns reference missing tools" ([Manus](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)). Keep all 14 tools constant and gate by state; 14 tools is also below the range where Anthropic says deferred loading helps much ([Anthropic, "Advanced tool use"](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/advanced-tool-use)).
6. **Cost is not the lever, and trimming can break caching.** A 25k-token prompt is about $0.0025 per message on Haiku 5.5 ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)); the case for decomposition is the instruction-count and length degradation evidence ([IFScale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538); [Levy et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14848)). On Haiku 4.5 the minimum cacheable prompt is 4,096 tokens, so a core pruned below that silently stops caching ([Anthropic prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)).
7. **Validators need three things the plan did not specify.** The retry message must list the admissible values (14/50 → 36/50 vs near-baseline for location-plus-value alone) ([VeriHarness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14167)), must be phrased as an external verdict because of the 64.5% self-attribution blind spot ([Self-Correction Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02778)), and must stop after one retry since gains concentrate on the first and "retry confusion" appears at ~6% ([arXiv 2601.11637](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.11637); [arXiv 2609.28488](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28488)).
8. **Structured output does not validate content, and Anthropic's schema subset is narrower than expected.** No `minimum`/`maximum`/`minLength`/`maxLength`, refusals and `max_tokens` bypass the schema, schema changes invalidate the prompt cache, and citations cannot be combined with structured outputs ([Anthropic structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs); [Anthropic citations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/citations)). Only "URL last" becomes structural.
9. **Sub-agents do not belong on the reply path.** Multi-agent systems use ~15× chat tokens ([Anthropic multi-agent](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/built-multi-agent-research-system)), every multi-agent variant degraded sequential tasks by 39–70% in Google's 180-configuration study ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/towards-a-science-of-scaling-agent-systems-when-and-why-agent-systems-work/)), and vendors run inline side models, not loops ([fin.ai](https://fin.ai/cx-models)). The call-log summary is one bounded worker call, ideally precomputed.
10. **A one-run comparison of 65 cases proves little.** SE ≈ 5 points at 80% pass; three repetitions and pairing are required to see ~7-point differences, and temperature must not be lowered to fake stability ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1)).
11. **A Haiku-class LLM judge is unreliable for tone.** Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 showed 23.8–46.2% position-swap consistency and were fooled by padded answers 91.3% of the time ([MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)); GPT-4.1-mini collapsed to one class (kappa 0.000) under a weak judge prompt ([Nubank](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08867v2)). Use a Sonnet-class pairwise judge, both orders, validated against labels, non-gating.
12. **Automatic prompt optimization is cheap but regresses on tiny sets.** A GEPA `light` run is ~500 metric calls ([DSPy gepa.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/stanfordnlp/dspy/main/dspy/teleprompt/gepa/gepa.py)), but with 20/10 examples Willison saw +5 on train and −10 on held-out, and metric bugs dominated ([Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/2/dspy-datasette-agent-prompts/)); the strongest support-domain result is on judge prompts, not reply prompts ([Nubank](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08867v2)).
13. **No tracing standard records which rules loaded.** OTel GenAI and the four tracing vendors link a generation to one whole prompt version at most; section-level attribution is a custom attribute, and model self-reported `applied_rule_ids` are hints, not evidence ([OTel GenAI spans](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions/v1.37.0/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-spans.md); [Turpin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388)).
14. **A planning/todo tool is not worth building.** The one team that measured it removed it from the default after evals showed no significant gain ([LangChain](https://langchain.com/blog/deep-agents-v0-7)).
15. **Order within a module matters even after decomposition.** IFScale's primacy bias means later instructions are violated more ([IFScale](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.11538)), so non-negotiables go first in each module.
16. **Disclosure is a regulatory requirement, not a style choice.** EU AI Act Art. 50(1) applies from 2026-08-02 and Utah SB 226 from 2025-05-07; the safe-harbor pattern is self-identification at the outset and throughout ([artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/); [Utah SB 226](https://le.utah.gov/Session/2025/bills/enrolled/SB0226.pdf)).
