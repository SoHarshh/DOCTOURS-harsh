# Escalation and handoff: how customer-facing AI agents decide to defer to a human, what the handoff looks like, how it is measured, and the academic framing (incl. Sierra's tau-bench family)

Research date: 2026-10-11. Scope: escalation design for a TypeScript SMS "patient coordinator" on a hosted Haiku-class model, no fine-tuning, ~10 developer-hours. The build must set `escalate=true` on explicit human requests and on requests no tool/rule can satisfy (card charges, moving paid money, contacting a clinic to hold a date), reply with one short sentence, and stop. Success is measured as escalation precision/recall on a tagged test set.

Notation used throughout: **false handoff** = escalated when the bot could have handled it (precision loss, wastes human time); **missed handoff** = handled when it should have escalated (recall loss, trust failure).

---

## Which escalation designs exist in products and research, and which should we try? (Method cards and recommendation)

### Takeaway
Every real product examined (Intercom Fin, Zendesk, Decagon, Forethought, Ada) and the tau-bench family use a **hybrid**: deterministic triggers and flow steps own the explicit/high-stakes cases and the mechanics of the handoff (message, routing, stop), while a model decides the judgment cases against a written policy. Nobody relies on raw model confidence as the primary gate, and the two sources that measured it (Xiong et al. 2024; Bachar et al. 2026) found verbalized/log-prob confidence to be overconfident or over-escalating. The one published prompt-only escalation classifier (Gusto, 2026) landed at ~90% precision and ~90% recall before any training, which is the realistic ceiling for a 10-hour prompt-plus-rules build.

### Cited Findings (method cards)

#### Card 1 — Hard-trigger rule list in code (explicit human request + enumerated "cannot do" actions)
- **One line:** Deterministic checks run before/alongside the model: explicit human-request phrases, and a fixed list of actions the system cannot perform, each mapped to `escalate=true` with a reason code.
- **Sources:** Intercom "How to automatically escalate conversations" (help article, updated 2026-08-31) — [intercom.com](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations); Zendesk "Transfer to agent" flow step and Pass Control API (help article) — [support.zendesk.com](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/9755691894298); Tian Pan, "Human-AI handoff escalation criteria" (practitioner blog, 2026-04-20) — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/04/20/human-ai-handoff-escalation-criteria).
- **Claims:** Intercom's default behavior escalates immediately when "a customer clearly asks for a human," and its data-driven **Escalation Rules** (e.g., Sentiment = Negative, Issue Type = Bug, order total above a threshold, `VIP_customer = true`) fire *before* Fin answers: "When a rule matches, Fin doesn't answer the question and hands off instead" — [Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations). Zendesk implements handoff as a deterministic "Transfer to agent" step in Flow Builder; "the Pass Control API call is always in charge to hand off control of the conversation from one system to another" and can carry metadata that populates ticket fields — [Zendesk](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/9755691894298). Tian Pan recommends listing emotional and complexity markers as "binary triggers that override the confidence threshold entirely," and calls letting the model judge its own failures "a structurally bad design" — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/04/20/human-ai-handoff-escalation-criteria).
- **Reported numbers:** None on trigger-list precision/recall. Intercom's guardrail set (no two consecutive offers, no first-message escalation unless a guideline allows) is rule-based even in its ML system — [fin.ai research, 2025-09-11](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/).
- **Effort:** ~1.5 h. Build: a `hardTriggers(message, toolPlan)` function returning `{escalate, reason}` for (a) human-request regex/phrase list in EN (+ any patient languages), (b) model-emitted intent in an `unsupported_action` enum (`charge_card`, `move_paid_money`, `clinic_hold_date`).
- **Risks:** Phrase lists miss paraphrases and non-English; over-broad keyword rules ("agent", "support") cause false handoffs — Intercom treats those words as *ambiguous* and only offers (see Card 3).
- **Verdict:** **Try first.** It is the only design that gives near-100% recall on the "I demand a human" class at zero model cost, and every vendor examined has an equivalent.

#### Card 2 — Escalation as an explicit tool/field whose customer-facing message is gated by code (the tau-bench pattern)
- **One line:** The model's only way to escalate is to call `transfer_to_human_agents(summary)` (or set a structured `escalate` field); the fixed handoff sentence is emitted by code only after that call is recorded.
- **Sources:** tau-bench airline policy (GitHub, 2024) — [raw wiki.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/wiki.md); tau-bench transfer tool source — [transfer_to_human_agents.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/tools/transfer_to_human_agents.py); tau2-bench telecom policy (GitHub, 2025) — [main_policy.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/telecom/main_policy.md); tau-bench paper (arXiv 2406.12045, 2024-06-17) — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045).
- **Claims:** tau-bench's policy rule is "You should transfer the user to a human agent if and only if the request cannot be handled within the scope of your actions" — [wiki.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/wiki.md). The tool is `transfer_to_human_agents` with one required string parameter `summary` ("A summary of the user's issue.") and returns the literal `"Transfer successful"`; its description restricts use to when the user explicitly asks for a human or the agent cannot resolve with available tools — [transfer_to_human_agents.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/tools/transfer_to_human_agents.py). tau2's telecom policy adds ordering and a fixed message: "first make a tool call to transfer_to_human_agents, and then send the message" `YOU ARE BEING TRANSFERRED TO A HUMAN AGENT. PLEASE HOLD ON.`, plus "You should try your best to resolve the issue for the user before transferring" — [main_policy.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/telecom/main_policy.md). tau-bench lists `transfer_to_human_agents` among the non-database APIs and counts 15 tools in retail (7 write), 13 in airline (6 write) — [arXiv 2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1).
- **Reported numbers:** The tau papers do not report transfer rates or score escalation separately ("none reported") — [arXiv 2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1). In tau2 airline, 1 of the ~49 inspected tasks (task 13) expects `transfer_to_human_agents`; the user instruction is "If the agent says that the change is not possible, you ask to be transferred" — [tasks.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/airline/tasks.json).
- **Effort:** ~2 h. Build: add `request_human` to the tool list (or an `escalate: {reason}` field in the structured reply), persist the flag, and have code (not the model) send the single-sentence handoff; drop any model text in the same turn.
- **Risks:** tau-bench shows models follow policy documents inconsistently (pass^8 < 25% for gpt-4o retail) — [Sierra blog, 2024-06-20](https://sierra.ai/blog/benchmarking-ai-agents) — so the "if and only if" rule alone will both over- and under-fire; it must be paired with Card 1 and Card 7.
- **Verdict:** **Try first.** It is the published pattern for making escalation an auditable action, and it structurally prevents "pretend escalation" because the customer-facing sentence only exists if the tool call was recorded.

#### Card 3 — Two-stage "offer, then escalate" for ambiguous requests (Intercom Fin)
- **One line:** Clear human requests escalate immediately; ambiguous signals (keyword "agent"/"support", asking how to contact support, frustration, repeating three times, first-turn escalation) produce an offer, and a repeat trigger after an offer escalates.
- **Sources:** Intercom help article (updated 2026-08-31) — [intercom.com](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations); fin.ai research post (2025-09-11) — [fin.ai](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/); Intercom community thread (2026-07-13) — [community.intercom.com](https://community.intercom.com/train-fin-94/overriding-fin-s-default-connect-to-human-prompt-to-check-for-existing-issue-context-first-15011).
- **Claims:** Fin "won't make two offers back-to-back. If a trigger fires again right after an offer, Fin escalates directly." Offers trigger when a customer "asks how to contact support, shows anger, uses an unclear keyword like 'agent' or 'support,' repeats themselves across three turns, or tries to escalate right after Fin's introduction" — [Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations). Intercom's production model makes a three-way decision per turn: escalate immediately / offer to escalate / let Fin answer — [fin.ai research](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/). The default offer text includes "Would you like me to connect you to a human agent?" — [community post](https://community.intercom.com/train-fin-94/overriding-fin-s-default-connect-to-human-prompt-to-check-for-existing-issue-context-first-15011).
- **Reported numbers:** No separate precision/recall for the offer class ("none reported").
- **Effort:** ~1.5 h. Build: a third output class `offer_human` with a code-owned one-line offer; state flag `offerMade` to force escalation on the next trigger.
- **Risks:** Adds a turn for genuinely urgent patients; the brief says "I demand to talk to a human" must escalate at once, so the offer must be reserved for ambiguous phrasing only.
- **Verdict:** **Try if time.** It is the main lever vendors use to cut false handoffs without lowering recall, but the brief's test set may not reward it.

#### Card 4 — Dedicated escalation-decision call with guideline citation (separate from the answer generation)
- **One line:** A small, separate model call takes the transcript plus the business's escalation guidelines and returns `{decision, reason_category, cited_guideline}`; the answer-generating call does not decide escalation.
- **Sources:** fin.ai research, "To escalate or not to escalate" (2025-09-11, Ramil Yarullin) — [fin.ai](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/); Gusto Engineering, "From prompt to classifier" (2026-06-29) via daily.dev summary — [daily.dev](https://daily.dev/posts/from-prompt-to-classifier-a-production-case-study-6p2dazqlv) (original at engineering.gusto.com returned HTTP 403 when fetched).
- **Claims:** Intercom's decision model inputs are "the conversation history and business-defined escalation guidelines (e.g., 'Escalate immediately if the user expresses anger about billing')"; it predicts a 3-way escalation decision, one of 8 reason categories, and a multi-label guideline citation; guideline-bearing conversations were 75% of traffic — [fin.ai](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/). Intercom started with an LLM-prompt system and moved to a ModernBERT encoder, keeping a hybrid where "the custom model handles ~90% of cases at >98% accuracy; the remaining ~10% (mostly very long or complex inputs) go to an LLM" — [fin.ai](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/). Gusto's LLM-prompt baseline for an AI-to-human escalation classifier measured about 90% precision and 90% recall before training a BERT model — [daily.dev summary](https://daily.dev/posts/from-prompt-to-classifier-a-production-case-study-6p2dazqlv).
- **Reported numbers:** Intercom: 97.4% escalation accuracy, 97% reason accuracy, 98.7% citation AUC; A/B test raised resolution rate (p < 0.01), cut escalation-detection latency 0.5 s, cut cost per resolution ~3%; precision/recall not reported — [fin.ai](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/). Gusto: prompt ~90/90 → BERT ~94% precision / ~93% recall on ~3,500 labeled turns, threshold tuned to recall ≥ 0.90 — [daily.dev](https://daily.dev/posts/from-prompt-to-classifier-a-production-case-study-6p2dazqlv).
- **Effort:** ~2 h. Build: a `decideEscalation(transcript, guidelines[])` Haiku call with a strict JSON schema (`decision ∈ {escalate, offer, answer}`, `reason ∈ enum`, `cited_rule_ids[]`), run before answer generation; log citations for the eval set.
- **Risks:** Doubles model calls per inbound SMS; a prompt-only decider plateaus near 90/90 per Gusto; the decision model must still be overridden by Card 1 hard triggers.
- **Verdict:** **Try if time** (do it after Cards 1, 2, 7, 9). It is the closest no-fine-tuning analogue of what Intercom shipped, and the citation field gives the eval harness a reason label for every decision.

#### Card 5 — Enumerated "cannot do" list plus a reason enum (detecting "no tool can do this")
- **One line:** The prompt and the escalation tool schema enumerate the actions the system cannot perform; the model must pick a reason code, and code validates that the reason matches a real gap.
- **Sources:** Tian Pan, "The Escalation Protocol" (2026-04-10) — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-10-escalation-protocol-agent-to-human-handoffs); Decagon platform page (vendor docs, undated) — [decagon.ai](https://decagon.ai/modules/decagon-platform); Decagon AOP page — [decagon.ai](https://decagon.ai/resources/from-sops-to-agent-operating-procedures); tau-bench policy — [wiki.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/wiki.md).
- **Claims:** Tian Pan ranks "task-level signals" as the most reliable triggers: "contradictory data from two tool calls, missing required fields, financial thresholds, or compliance-flagged keywords," ahead of behavioral signals and ensemble disagreement, and explicitly argues against raw model confidence as the main gate — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-10-escalation-protocol-agent-to-human-handoffs). Decagon's pipeline is "extract the intent ... select the right AOP to run. If no AOP matches, a fallback mechanism uses retrieval-augmented generation" — i.e., a missing procedure falls back to RAG, not to a human — [decagon.ai](https://decagon.ai/modules/decagon-platform); AOPs "cover when to escalate a situation, much like the procedures used to train human agents" — [decagon.ai](https://decagon.ai/resources/from-sops-to-agent-operating-procedures). tau-bench ties the scope test to the tool set: transfer "if and only if the request cannot be handled within the scope of your actions" and "You should not provide any information, knowledge, or procedures not provided by the user or available tools" — [wiki.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/wiki.md).
- **Reported numbers:** None for capability-gap detection accuracy ("none reported").
- **Effort:** ~1 h (shared with Card 2). Build: `reason` enum `{human_requested, payment_card_charge, move_paid_funds, clinic_hold_request, other_out_of_scope}`; a prompt block "Actions you cannot perform (escalate instead): ..." listing the three named gaps and the 14 tools' boundaries.
- **Risks:** The `other_out_of_scope` bucket is where false handoffs collect; Intercom warns "Broad guidance increases escalations and lowers resolution rates" — [Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations).
- **Verdict:** **Try first.** The brief's three named gaps are exactly the kind of fixed, nameable actions that vendors encode as rules rather than leave to judgment.

#### Card 6 — Confidence-threshold gating (verbalized or log-prob confidence)
- **One line:** Escalate when the model's self-reported or token-level confidence falls below a threshold.
- **Sources:** Xiong et al., "Can LLMs Express Their Uncertainty?" (ICLR 2024; arXiv 2306.13063) — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063); Bachar et al., "LLM Performance Predictors: Learning When to Escalate" (AAMAS 2026; arXiv 2601.07006, 2026-01-11) — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07006v1); Tian Pan (2026-04-20) — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/04/20/human-ai-handoff-escalation-criteria); Alhena vendor blog (2026-04-27) — [alhena.ai](https://alhena.ai/blog/ai-escalation-rate-optimization-ecommerce/).
- **Claims:** LLMs "tend to be overconfident" when verbalizing confidence; white-box methods beat black-box ones only narrowly, AUROC 0.522–0.605 — [Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063). Confidence baselines such as max-softmax-probability and entropy "tend to over-escalate, triggering unnecessary human reviews" in a hybrid human-AI moderation setting — [Bachar et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07006v1). Tian Pan calls confidence "necessary but insufficient as the sole escalation signal" and suggests tiered floors (90–95% for regulated high-stakes, 70–80% standard) only as a graded signal under hard triggers — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/04/20/human-ai-handoff-escalation-criteria). Alhena (vendor) recommends confidence-threshold tuning per category — [alhena.ai](https://alhena.ai/blog/ai-escalation-rate-optimization-ecommerce/).
- **Reported numbers:** Bachar et al.'s trained meta-model cut expected cost 22–71% versus the best baseline across models, with the optimal trust threshold near 0.5 on a validation sweep of [0.35, 0.70] — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07006v1).
- **Effort:** ~1 h for a verbalized-confidence field; log-probs are not exposed on the hosted Claude API in a usable way for this (not verified in this research; treat as a constraint to check).
- **Risks:** Both measured sources show the raw signal is poorly calibrated; a threshold will mostly add false handoffs.
- **Verdict:** **Skip** as a primary gate; optionally log a verbalized confidence for analysis only.

#### Card 7 — Stop-after-handoff guard and code-owned handoff sentence
- **One line:** Once `escalate=true` is persisted, the bot stops generating for that conversation and all inbound messages route to the human queue; the handoff sentence is a code constant, never model text.
- **Sources:** Intercom community threads on Fin replying after handoff (2023–2026) — [WhatsApp thread](https://community.intercom.com/workflows-7/fin-ai-agent-issues-on-the-whatsapp-channel-12977), [Fin replying to team member, 2026](https://community.intercom.com/train-fin-94/fin-replying-to-team-member-15067); Macha third-party blog — [getmacha.com](https://www.getmacha.com/blog/intercom-fin-handoff-setup); Intercom help article — [intercom.com](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations); tau2 telecom policy — [main_policy.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/telecom/main_policy.md).
- **Claims:** Intercom users report that after a teammate takes over, "as soon as the customer responds again, Fin jumps back in and interrupts" — [community](https://community.intercom.com/workflows-7/fin-ai-agent-issues-on-the-whatsapp-channel-12977); a third-party guide states "Fin keeps replying until a teammate sends a customer-facing reply, so reassigning the conversation, changing ticket state or leaving an internal note does not end Fin's session" — [getmacha.com](https://www.getmacha.com/blog/intercom-fin-handoff-setup) (not verified against Intercom docs). Intercom's Escalation Rules "post a brief default message such as 'Let me connect you with a teammate' before routing" — a system-generated line, not model text — [Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations). tau2's policy fixes the post-transfer message verbatim and orders it after the tool call — [main_policy.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/telecom/main_policy.md).
- **Reported numbers:** None.
- **Effort:** ~1 h. Build: `conversation.state = 'HANDED_OFF'` check at the top of the inbound handler; constant `HANDOFF_SMS`; a human "release" action returns the state to `BOT`.
- **Risks:** Patients who text again after handoff get silence unless the human queue is staffed; consider a code-owned "a coordinator will reply during office hours" auto-ack.
- **Verdict:** **Try first.** This is the cheapest part of the build and is the only way to guarantee the bot never "pretends"; Intercom's own community shows what happens without it.

#### Card 8 — Structured handoff payload for the human
- **One line:** The human receives a validated object (goal, state, evidence with provenance, what was tried, the exact question needing a human, trace id) plus the transcript, not a free-text summary alone.
- **Sources:** Decagon glossary, "AI agent handoff schema" (vendor, undated) — [decagon.ai](https://decagon.ai/glossary/what-is-an-ai-agent-handoff-schema); Tian Pan (2026-04-10) — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-10-escalation-protocol-agent-to-human-handoffs); Intercom community "AI Summaries" (user post) — [community.intercom.com](https://community.intercom.com/fin-tips-from-the-intercom-team-85/save-time-and-avoid-repetition-with-ai-summaries-10554); Intercom marketing page — [intercom.com](https://www.intercom.com/conversational-ai); tau-bench tool — [transfer_to_human_agents.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/tools/transfer_to_human_agents.py).
- **Claims:** Decagon's schema fields: objective and current state ("what is done, what is blocked, and what comes next"), evidence, provenance ("a knowledge base article ID, a timestamped tool response, or an unverified customer statement"), confidence, authorization scope, attempts and exclusions, schema version; it warns "free-text summaries drop provenance" and recommends validating payloads at the boundary — [decagon.ai](https://decagon.ai/glossary/what-is-an-ai-agent-handoff-schema). Tian Pan's payload: action history with per-step reasoning and data, current context (goal, determinations, the specific ambiguity that triggered escalation), a structured problem statement ("the exact question the human must answer"), and a `trace_id` — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-10-escalation-protocol-agent-to-human-handoffs). Intercom hands off "with the full conversation history attached" and an optional "Add summary note" workflow action writes an AI summary as an internal note at the handoff point — [intercom.com](https://www.intercom.com/conversational-ai), [community](https://community.intercom.com/fin-tips-from-the-intercom-team-85/save-time-and-avoid-repetition-with-ai-summaries-10554). tau-bench passes a single `summary` string — [transfer_to_human_agents.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/tools/transfer_to_human_agents.py).
- **Reported numbers:** Tian Pan reports manual prep time falling from ~15 minutes with a raw transcript to ~30 seconds with a structured payload (~97%), unsourced — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-10-escalation-protocol-agent-to-human-handoffs).
- **Effort:** ~1 h. Build: `HandoffPayload { reason, patientGoal, toolCallsMade[], unresolvedQuestion, transcriptRef, traceId }` written at escalation time.
- **Risks:** Model-written summary fields can hallucinate; keep tool-derived fields code-populated and mark the model's intent reading as "unverified."
- **Verdict:** **Try first** (minimal version). The reason code and tool-call list are free since the system already has them.

#### Card 9 — Outcome taxonomy and abstention-style metrics
- **One line:** Define outcomes the way Zendesk/Intercom/Ada do (resolved vs handed off vs unresolved) and score escalation with the abstention confusion matrix (precision, recall, over-abstention rate).
- **Sources:** Wen et al., "Know Your Limits: A Survey of Abstention in LLMs" (arXiv 2407.18418, 2024-07; TACL 2025) — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3); Zendesk "About automated resolution tiers" (effective 2026-05-18) — [support.zendesk.com](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/9570369117338); Intercom "Fin AI Agent outcomes" — [fin.ai](https://fin.ai/help/en/articles/13975800-fin-ai-agent-outcomes); Ada docs on automated resolution rate — [docs.ada.cx](https://docs.ada.cx/docs/generative/measure-success/understand-and-improve-your-ai-agent-s-automated-resolution-rate).
- **Claims:** Wen et al. define a five-cell matrix (N1 correct answer / N2 wrong answer when no abstention expected; N3 abstained when not expected; N4 answered wrongly when abstention expected; N5 abstained when expected) and metrics: Abstention Precision = N5/(N3+N5), Abstention Recall = N5/(N2+N4+N5), over-abstention on safe prompts ARSP = N3/(N1+N2+N3), Effective Reliability = (N1−N2−N4)/total; they warn "Over-abstention occurs when models abstain unnecessarily" and that safety/self-check instructions make models "overly cautious" — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3). Zendesk's tiers: "Assisted escalation: The AI agent contributed to the interaction before a human agent completed the resolution"; "Contained resolution ... without the customer requesting further assistance"; "Verified resolution" requires an LLM check — [Zendesk](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/9570369117338).
- **Reported numbers:** See the quantitative table (Intercom 24 h assumed-resolution rule; Zendesk 2 h/72 h idle windows; Ada 24 h/72 h).
- **Effort:** ~1.5 h. Build: a test runner over the tagged set that emits precision, recall, ARSP-style false-handoff rate on handleable messages, and a per-reason-code breakdown.
- **Risks:** None technical; the labeling of "should escalate" must be done by the team before the metric is meaningful.
- **Verdict:** **Try first.** It is required by the brief and the formulas are published.

#### Card 10 — Self-consistency vote on the escalation decision
- **One line:** Sample the escalation decision k times and escalate on majority (or on any vote, for recall-first).
- **Sources:** Wen et al. survey (inference-time methods) — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3); Xiong et al. (consistency-based confidence) — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063); tau-bench pass^k — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1).
- **Claims:** The survey lists "sampling multiple responses and abstaining when they disagree" as a training-free abstention method — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3); Xiong et al. find consistency-based methods help mitigate overconfidence — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063). tau-bench's pass^k shows single-run agent decisions are unstable (pass^8 < 25% vs pass^1 61.2% for gpt-4o retail) — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1).
- **Reported numbers:** None specific to escalation.
- **Effort:** ~0.5 h on top of Card 4 (loop k=3 at temperature > 0). SMS is asynchronous, so the latency is tolerable.
- **Risks:** 3x cost per decision; majority voting lowers recall if the "escalate" class is the minority vote — use "any vote escalates" for the recall-first setting.
- **Verdict:** **Try if time.** Cheap variance reduction; only worth it if the eval shows flip-flopping on the same message.

#### Card 11 — Trained escalation classifier (Intercom ModernBERT, Gusto BERT, Bachar et al. meta-model)
- **One line:** A small supervised model replaces or precedes the LLM decision.
- **Sources:** [fin.ai research, 2025-09-11](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/); [Gusto via daily.dev, 2026-06-29](https://daily.dev/posts/from-prompt-to-classifier-a-production-case-study-6p2dazqlv); [Bachar et al., 2026-01-11](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07006v1).
- **Claims/numbers:** Intercom trained on 4M examples; Gusto on ~3,500 labeled turns reaching ~94% P / ~93% R; Bachar et al. cut cost 22–71% versus confidence baselines (sources above).
- **Effort:** Out of scope (requires training and labeled data beyond 10 h).
- **Verdict:** **Skip** (violates the no-fine-tuning constraint), but adopt two of its practices: label ~a few hundred real turns and tune the decision threshold toward a recall target (Gusto used recall ≥ 0.90).

### Inferences (recommendation: code triggers vs model judgment vs hybrid)
- **Recommendation: hybrid with code as the authority.** Code owns (a) the explicit human-request trigger, (b) the three named unsupported actions, (c) the handoff sentence, (d) the stop state, and (e) the metrics. The model owns only the residual judgment ("this request needs something none of my tools do") and must express it through a constrained `reason` enum so that every escalation is attributable and testable. This mirrors Intercom (rules fire before Fin answers; guardrails are hard-coded even in its ML decider), Zendesk (deterministic transfer step and Pass Control API), and the tau-bench pattern (escalation is a tool, the message is fixed and ordered after the call).
- **Do not use model confidence as the gate.** Two measured sources (Xiong et al. 2024; Bachar et al. 2026) show it is overconfident or over-escalating; the one practitioner protocol with numbers (Tian Pan 2026) demotes it to a secondary signal.
- **Expect ~90/90 from a prompt-only decider** (Gusto's measured baseline) and plan the test set so the hard-trigger class (explicit requests, named unsupported actions) is scored separately from the judgment class; the former should be near 100% recall by construction, so overall recall is dominated by the judgment class.
- **Bias the judgment class toward recall but monitor ARSP.** The brief's cost asymmetry (false handoffs waste human time at scale; missed handoffs are the trust failure) matches Alhena's ecommerce guidance that "recall usually matters more," but Intercom's data says broad "escalate if unsure" instructions inflate false handoffs, so write the out-of-scope rule narrowly (the three named actions plus "any request to change money or bookings already paid").
- **Build order for 10 hours:** Card 1 (1.5 h) → Card 2 + Card 5 (3 h) → Card 7 (1 h) → Card 8 minimal (1 h) → Card 9 (1.5 h) = 8 h; remaining 2 h for Card 4 or Card 3 depending on which error class dominates in the first eval run.

### Gaps
- No vendor publishes precision/recall for its escalation decision; Intercom reports accuracy only (97.4%), and Gusto's numbers come via a secondary summary because the original post returned HTTP 403.
- No published study isolates the accuracy of "capability gap" detection (Card 5) from "customer asked for a human" detection.

### Takeaway
Intercom and Zendesk publish the most concrete criteria: Intercom escalates by default on a clear human request, strong frustration, or a loop, layered with data-driven Rules and natural-language Guidance; Zendesk makes handoff a flow step and excludes any escalated conversation from "automated resolution." Sierra publishes nothing concrete about its production handoff.

## What do published handoff messages look like, and how do products avoid the bot claiming it escalated when nothing happened?

### Takeaway
Published handoff messages are one short, system-owned sentence ("Let me connect you with a teammate"; "YOU ARE BEING TRANSFERRED TO A HUMAN AGENT. PLEASE HOLD ON."). The anti-"pretend escalation" pattern is structural: the message is sent by the platform (Intercom Rules, Zendesk flow step) or is required to follow a recorded tool call whose return value confirms the transfer (tau-bench). Products that let the model keep talking after handoff (Intercom's reported behavior) generate the exact failure the brief wants to avoid.

### Cited Findings
- Intercom Escalation Rules "post a brief default message such as 'Let me connect you with a teammate' before routing"; guidance affects "whether it explains why it's escalating"; post-escalation messaging is configured in workflows — [Intercom help](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations).
- Fin's default offer wording includes "Would you like me to connect you to a human agent?" and an invitation to keep working with Fin — [community, 2026-07-13](https://community.intercom.com/train-fin-94/overriding-fin-s-default-connect-to-human-prompt-to-check-for-existing-issue-context-first-15011).
- Fin's automatic handoff message is reported as "something like 'A colleague from our support team will take a look...'"; it can be reshaped via Guidance, and one user reported Intercom support disabling it with a "Killswitch" — [community, 2026-08-24](https://community.intercom.com/fin-faqs-97/how-to-disable-edit-fin-s-automatic-handoff-message-15344).
- tau2 telecom: "first make a tool call to transfer_to_human_agents, and then send the message" `YOU ARE BEING TRANSFERRED TO A HUMAN AGENT. PLEASE HOLD ON.`; the agent "should not respond to the user while making a tool call" — [main_policy.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/telecom/main_policy.md). The tool returns `"Transfer successful"` — [transfer_to_human_agents.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/tools/transfer_to_human_agents.py).
- tau2 retail policy (as rendered in the paper) requires calling the transfer tool and then telling the user they are being transferred — [arXiv 2506.07982](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07982).
- Zendesk's "Transfer to agent" step and Pass Control API make the transfer a platform action independent of model text — [Zendesk](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/9755691894298).
- Failure evidence: "as soon as the customer responds again, Fin jumps back in and interrupts" — [Intercom community](https://community.intercom.com/workflows-7/fin-ai-agent-issues-on-the-whatsapp-channel-12977); a 2026 thread on Fin replying after a teammate answered — [community](https://community.intercom.com/train-fin-94/fin-replying-to-team-member-15067).
- Tian Pan recommends explicit structured resume signals (approval, rejection with reason, modified parameters) "rather than inferring resolution from a new message," and monitoring escalation rates to catch "overconfident non-escalation" — [tianpan.co, 2026-04-10](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-10-escalation-protocol-agent-to-human-handoffs).
- Intercom's billing rule "You will never be charged for an outcome that didn't happen" implies the platform records handoff as a discrete event, not as model text — [fin.ai outcomes](https://fin.ai/help/en/articles/13975800-fin-ai-agent-outcomes).

### Inferences
- The brief's "one short sentence, then stop" is exactly the published shape; the sentence should be a code constant chosen by the team (e.g., "A coordinator will take over this conversation and reply to you here."), never model-generated, and only sent after the escalation record is persisted.
- Because the handoff is a state transition, "pretending" is prevented by construction: if the model's reply contains language like "I've connected you" without the escalation flag, treat it as a defect in the eval (a regex check on outbound text for "connect you / transferred / a colleague will" when `escalate=false` is a cheap guard).
- The stop condition must be a persisted conversation state, not a prompt instruction; Intercom's community threads show prompt-level "don't reply" is unreliable.

### Gaps
- No vendor publishes the exact default handoff string in official docs; the two Intercom strings come from community posts.
- No source measures how often a bot falsely claims to have escalated ("none reported").

## What precision/recall tradeoffs do people choose for handoff, and what are the numbers on false-handoff cost?

### Takeaway
The only measured escalation-classifier numbers found are Gusto's (prompt baseline ~90% P/~90% R; trained model ~94% P/~93% R with the threshold tuned to recall ≥ 0.90) and Intercom's accuracy figures (97–98%, no P/R). Cost-of-false-handoff numbers are all vendor or practitioner estimates: $8–15 per false escalation vs $0.50–2 per AI resolution (Alhena, unsourced), Gartner-derived $13.50 agent-assisted vs $1.84 self-service (via Alhena), and "20–40% of escalations were unnecessary" (Tian Pan, unsourced). Target escalation rates cluster at 10–20% for simple support and 30–50% for regulated or medical categories.

### Cited Findings
- Gusto: LLM-prompt baseline "about 90% precision and recall"; ~3,500 labeled production turns; BERT classifier ~94% precision / ~93% recall; "threshold was tuned to a recall target of at least 0.90 rather than the default 0.5" — [daily.dev summary of Gusto Engineering, 2026-06-29](https://daily.dev/posts/from-prompt-to-classifier-a-production-case-study-6p2dazqlv) (original returned 403).
- Intercom: 97.4% escalation accuracy, 97% reason accuracy, 98.7% guideline-citation AUC; A/B: resolution rate up (p < 0.01), latency −0.5 s, cost per resolution −3%; "Not reported: precision, recall" — [fin.ai research, 2025-09-11](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/).
- Intercom framing of the two error costs: over-escalation "flood[s] support teams with unnecessary escalations," under-escalation "leaves users stuck without human help" — [fin.ai research](https://fin.ai/research/to-escalate-or-not-to-escalate-that-is-the-question/).
- Alhena (vendor, 2026-04-27): false escalation = "conversations the AI routed to a human agent that it could have resolved on its own"; missed escalation = tickets "the AI attempted to resolve that genuinely needed a human"; "$8 to $15 in agent handling time, versus $0.50 to $2 for an AI resolution" (no source given); Gartner-based $1.84 self-service vs $13.50 agent-assisted; illustration 250 false escalations × $12 ≈ $3,000/month; precision = share of escalations that truly needed a human, recall = share of needed escalations that were escalated; "recall usually matters more" for ecommerce; target ranges 12–18% (simple beauty) and 30–40% (luxury/regulated), "a medical device brand may need human review on about half of tickets" — [alhena.ai](https://alhena.ai/blog/ai-escalation-rate-optimization-ecommerce/).
- Tian Pan (2026-04-20): teams that review escalations quarterly "consistently find 20–40% of escalations were unnecessary" (no study cited) — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/04/20/human-ai-handoff-escalation-criteria). Tian Pan (2026-04-10): target 10–15% of cases escalated; 15–20%+ "unsustainable" — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-10-escalation-protocol-agent-to-human-handoffs). Tian Pan (2026-06-02): the deflection metric "does not penalize the agent for routing fixable problems into the human queue" — [tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-06-02-the-agent-escalation-policy-your-support-team-treated-as-the-new-front-line).
- Decagon: escalation rates "under 20% are considered healthy" — [decagon.ai glossary](https://decagon.ai/glossary/what-is-escalation-rate). Unthread: 15–20% baseline for mature service desks; ~$0.50 AI vs ~$6.00 human per interaction (vendor-sourced) — [unthread.io](https://unthread.io/blog/support-ticket-escalation-statistics/).
- Bachar et al. (AAMAS 2026): cost model C(τ) = c_mis·FP + (c_rev − c_mis)·TN + c_rev·FN with c_rev/c_mis ≈ 0.64; meta-model vs best baseline cost: gpt-4o-mini $132→$38 (−71%), gemini-2.0-flash-lite $74→$41 (−41%), gemini-2.5-flash-lite $122→$77 (−36%) on text moderation; $37→$22 (−41%), $42→$29 (−32%), $51→$40 (−22%) on multimodal; MSP/entropy baselines "tend to over-escalate"; threshold τ chosen on a 20% validation split by minimizing expected cost, optimum "consistently falls near the midpoint (0.5)" — [arxiv 2601.07006](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07006v1).
- Older classical baseline for escalation prediction (ESSMArT, 2018): precision 0.86, recall 0.53, F1 0.65 — [arxiv 1808.03796](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.03796).

### Inferences
- A defensible v1 target for this build is Gusto's prompt baseline (~90/90) overall, with near-100% recall on the hard-trigger class; anything materially below 90% recall on the judgment class should trigger adding Card 4.
- Set the operating point by a cost ratio the team states explicitly (human minutes per false handoff vs the trust cost of a missed one); Bachar et al. show the optimum is found by sweeping a threshold on a held-out split, which the tagged test set enables.
- Report escalation rate alongside P/R; a healthy range for a medical-adjacent, high-stakes SMS flow is plausibly well above the 10–20% "simple support" band, so do not treat a 30%+ rate as a defect by itself.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of support-desk false-handoff rates or costs; all dollar figures are vendor estimates.
- No published P/R from Intercom, Zendesk, Ada, Decagon, Forethought, or Sierra.

## How is "no tool can do this" detected: static lists, model self-assessment, confidence thresholds, or tool-call failure?

### Takeaway
In products and benchmarks, "out of scope" is detected by scoping the policy to the tool set (tau-bench's "if and only if the request cannot be handled within the scope of your actions") and by static procedure/trigger lists (Intercom Rules, Decagon AOP escalation paths, Tian Pan's task-level signals); confidence thresholds are secondary at best. No source uses tool-call *failure* as the primary escalation trigger; Decagon's "no AOP matches" path falls back to RAG, not to a human.

### Cited Findings
- tau-bench: transfer "if and only if the request cannot be handled within the scope of your actions"; "You should not provide any information, knowledge, or procedures not provided by the user or available tools" — [wiki.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/wiki.md). - tau2 airline task 13 encodes the pattern "agent refuses (origin/destination can't be modified) → user insists → transfer" — [tasks.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/airline/tasks.json).
- Intercom Rules fire on data attributes before the answer; Guidance is natural-language and should target "the narrowest possible situations" — [Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/12396892-how-to-automatically-escalate-conversations). - Decagon: "If no AOP matches, a fallback mechanism uses retrieval-augmented generation" — [decagon.ai](https://decagon.ai/modules/decagon-platform).
- Tian Pan: task-level signals (contradictory tool data, missing required fields, financial thresholds, compliance keywords) are "most reliable"; behavioral signals (repeated identical API calls, scope creep); ensemble disagreement; raw confidence is "systematically overoptimistic" — [tianpan.co, 2026-04-10](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-10-escalation-protocol-agent-to-human-handoffs). - Confidence-based gating: verbalized confidence is overconfident (Xiong et al.) — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063); MSP/entropy over-escalate (Bachar et al.) — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07006v1).
- Learning-to-Defer "with advice" (2026-03) frames the decision as two-step: defer to an expert, or first acquire extra information such as tool use — [arxiv 2603.14324](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.14324v1).

### Inferences
- For the three named gaps (card charge, moving paid money, clinic hold), a static list is both the published pattern and the highest-recall option: encode them as intents the model must map to a reason code, and have code assert that no tool in the 14-tool set covers them.
- Add "tool-call failure after one retry" as a *secondary* trigger with its own reason code so it can be measured separately; no source endorses it as primary, and it risks false handoffs on transient errors.
- The "insist after refusal" pattern (tau2 task 13) is worth a rule: if the bot has already said "I can't do X" and the patient repeats the request, escalate rather than refuse twice.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies how often capability-gap escalations are correct versus unnecessary.
- Tian Pan's self-assessment numbers (73% vs 35%) are cited without a retrievable primary source.

## Academic framing: selective prediction, learning to defer, and LLM abstention — what transfers to a rule-based support bot?

### Takeaway
Three literatures apply directly. Selective prediction (Geifman & El-Yaniv 2017) gives the risk–coverage curve: handoff rate is coverage, error-among-handled is risk. Learning to defer (Mozannar & Sontag 2020 and successors) separates the *rejector* from the *classifier* and treats deferral as cost-sensitive — the template for a code-owned decision function with an explicit cost ratio. The 2024–2025 abstention survey (Wen et al.) supplies the exact confusion-matrix metrics the brief needs (abstention precision/recall, over-abstention rate) and the warning that "escalate if unsure"-style instructions cause over-abstention. The fine-tuning parts do not transfer; the metrics, the rejector/classifier split, and the threshold-by-cost procedure do.

### Cited Findings
- Geifman & El-Yaniv, "Selective Classification for Deep Neural Networks" (arXiv 1705.08500, 2017-05): a selective classifier from a trained network lets a user "set a desired risk level" and rejects instances to meet it with high probability; selective classification trades coverage for risk; e.g., 2% top-5 error on ImageNet guaranteed with 99.9% probability at almost 60% coverage — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500).
- Mozannar & Sontag, "Consistent Estimators for Learning to Defer to an Expert" (ICML 2020; arXiv 2006.01862): a predictor "either make[s] a prediction or pass[es] the decision to a downstream expert"; a classifier paired with a rejector; reduced to cost-sensitive learning with a consistent surrogate loss generalizing cross-entropy — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01862).
- Successors: Verma & Nalisnick 2022 one-versus-all surrogate; Mozannar et al. 2023 (AISTATS) "Who should predict? Exact algorithms for learning to defer to humans"; Narasimhan et al. 2022 post-hoc estimators; Mao et al. 2024 realizable H-consistent losses — [arxiv 2407.13732](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.13732); multi-expert and online variants (Montreuil et al. 2025–2026) — [arxiv 2604.09414](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.09414), [arxiv 2605.12340](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.12340); L2D for extractive QA with LLMs (Montreuil et al., v3 Feb 2025) — [arxiv 2410.15761](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15761v3); "Learning-to-Defer with advice" (2026-03) adds a defer-or-acquire-information step — [arxiv 2603.14324](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.14324v1).
- Wen et al., "Know Your Limits: A Survey of Abstention in LLMs" (arXiv 2407.18418, July 2024; TACL 2025): "We define abstention as the refusal to answer a query"; metrics (N1..N5 matrix): Abstention Accuracy (N1+N5)/total; Abstention Precision N5/(N3+N5); Abstention Recall N5/(N2+N4+N5); Abstention F1; Coverage (N1+N2+N4)/total; Abstention Rate (N3+N5)/total; over-abstention on safe prompts ARSP = N3/(N1+N2+N3); Reliable Accuracy N1/(N1+N2+N4); Effective Reliability (N1−N2−N4)/total — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3).
- Wen et al. on over-abstention: "the 'self-check' technique can make LLMs overly cautious with benign inputs" (Varshney et al. 2023); excessive abstention tuning "lead[s] models to refuse legitimate requests"; need "to balance abstention with utility" — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3).
- Training-free abstention methods listed by the survey: prompting ("Answer the question only if answerable," few-shot abstain examples), self-consistency (abstain when samples disagree), calibration (temperature scaling), input processing — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18418v3).
- Xiong et al. (ICLR 2024): LLMs "tend to be overconfident" in verbalized confidence; white-box methods only narrowly better (AUROC 0.522–0.605); consistency-based aggregation helps — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063).
- Bachar et al. (AAMAS 2026): a supervised meta-model over LLM-output features enables "cost-aware selective classification, escalating high-risk cases and automating the rest" — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07006).

### Inferences
- **Map to the brief:** "should escalate" label = abstention expected. Then false handoff = N3, missed handoff = N4 (or N2+N4 if a wrong answer on a handleable message is also counted as a miss). Escalation precision = N5/(N3+N5); escalation recall = N5/(N4+N5) when the tagged set only labels escalation; ARSP = N3/(handleable messages) is the "wasted human time" rate the brief cares about.
- **Rejector/classifier split transfers:** the L2D framing says the deferral decision is its own function with its own loss. In a rule-based bot that means the escalation decision should be a separate, testable function (code + optional dedicated model call), not a side effect of the answer-generating prompt.
- **Threshold by cost transfers:** Bachar et al.'s procedure (sweep a threshold on a held-out split to minimize c_rev·escalations + c_mis·misses) can be applied to the judgment-class decider's output score or to the self-consistency vote count.
- **Fine-tuned surrogates do not transfer** under the no-fine-tuning constraint; neither do log-prob features unless the hosted API exposes them.
- **Over-abstention warning transfers verbatim:** a blanket "when unsure, hand off" instruction is the LLM equivalent of safety over-tuning and will inflate N3; keep the escalation instruction tied to enumerated conditions.

### Gaps
- No dedicated survey of learning-to-defer was found; the lineage is reconstructed from reference lists of 2024–2026 papers.
- No 2025 paper studies LLM *agents* deferring to humans mid-conversation; the closest are the moderation setting (Bachar et al.) and extractive QA (Montreuil et al.).

## Sierra's tau-bench (Yao et al. 2024) and tau2-bench (Barres et al. 2025): scoring, pass^k, policy-compliance findings, and anything about transfer-to-human

### Takeaway
tau-bench scores an agent by comparing the final database state to an annotated goal state (plus required output substrings), under a written policy and simulated user; pass^k measures the chance that *all* k trials succeed. The headline finding is inconsistency and weak policy following: gpt-4o reached 61.2% pass^1 on retail but under 25% at pass^8, and removing the policy from the prompt dropped airline pass^1 by ~22 points. Both benchmarks include a `transfer_to_human_agents` tool governed by an "if and only if out of scope" rule, but neither paper scores escalation separately; tau2 airline has one inspected task (of 50) whose correct outcome is a transfer.

### Cited Findings
- Definitions: pass^k = E_task[C(c,k)/C(n,k)], "the chance that all k i.i.d. task trials are successful, averaged across tasks"; pass@k = 1 − E_task[C(n−c,k)/C(n,k)]; pass^k "measures reliability and consistency" — [arXiv 2406.12045 v1, 2024-06-17](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1).
- Evaluation: reward r = r_action × r_output; the final database "must be identical to the unique ground-truth outcome database" and responses "must contain the required outputs as substrings"; the authors note r = 1 is "necessary but not sufficient" since an agent could skip required user confirmation — [arXiv 2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1).
- Scale: τ-retail 115 tasks, 15 tools (7 write, 8 non-write); τ-airline 50 tasks, 13 tools (6 write, 7 non-write) — [arXiv 2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1).
- Results (pass^1, retail/airline): gpt-4o 61.2/35.2; gpt-4-turbo 57.7/32.4; claude-3-opus 44.2/34.7; claude-3-sonnet 26.3/27.6; claude-3-haiku 19.0/14.4; gemini-1.5-pro 21.7/14.0; mistral-large 30.7/22.4; llama-3-70B 14.8/14.4 — [arXiv 2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1). gpt-4o retail pass^8 < 25% — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045); "a drop of roughly 60% from its pass^1 score" — [Sierra blog, 2024-06-20](https://sierra.ai/blog/benchmarking-ai-agents).
- Policy ablation: without the policy in the system prompt, gpt-4o airline pass^1 fell from 33.2 to 10.8 (−22.4) and retail from 61.2 to 56.8 — [arXiv 2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1).
- Failure analysis (40 failed gpt-4o retail trajectories; 4 attributed to user-simulator issues): "wrong decision" ~25% (misunderstands domain rules, wrong tool-call type); "wrong argument or information" roughly 55% (the HTML rendering of the figure is garbled); partial resolution of compound requests 19%; example policy violation: exchange tools "can only be called once" but the agent exchanged items one at a time — [arXiv 2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1). Sierra's summary: "Function-calling agents were weak at following the rules in policy documents"; main challenges are "following rules consistently, planning over long horizons, and focusing on the right information when conversations contain conflicting facts" — [sierra.ai](https://sierra.ai/blog/benchmarking-ai-agents).
- Escalation in tau-bench: `transfer_to_human_agents` is listed as a non-database API; both policies say to transfer "only when a request cannot be handled within the agent's scope"; "The results do not report transfer rates or evaluate escalation behavior" — [arXiv 2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1); tool source — [transfer_to_human_agents.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau-bench/main/tau_bench/envs/airline/tools/transfer_to_human_agents.py).
- tau2-bench (arXiv 2506.07982, 2025-06-09; Barres, Dong, Ray, Si, Narasimhan): adds dual control (user also has tools), a Telecom domain modeled as a Dec-POMDP, a compositional task generator, and a simulator tied to the environment; "significant performance drops when agents shift from no-user to dual-control" — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07982).
- tau2 numbers: gpt-4.1 pass^1 74% retail, 56% airline, 34% telecom; o4-mini telecom 42%; claude-3.7-sonnet telecom 49%; no-user → dual-control drop in telecom: gpt-4.1 −18 points, o4-mini −25 points ("around 20%"); performance "nearing zero above 7 actions in Default mode"; telecom pass^k "falls faster than airline as k increases"; telecom: 114 tasks sampled from 2,285 generated, 6 write + 7 read agent tools, 15 write + 15 read user tools; a workflow-style policy "slightly improved pass^1" in Default/No-User modes — [arXiv 2506.07982](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07982).
- tau2 user-simulator error rates (not agent errors): retail 40% total / 12% critical; airline 47% / 13%; telecom 16% / 6% — [arXiv 2506.07982](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07982).
- tau2 and transfer: the retail policy "requires calling a transfer tool and then telling the user they are being transferred"; in telecom, "transfer" is a separate category in the action-count analysis "for tasks that must be handed to a human"; the simulator prompt includes a transfer token — [arXiv 2506.07982](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07982). Telecom policy text: transfer "if and only if the request cannot be handled within the scope of your actions"; "try your best to resolve the issue for the user before transferring"; call the tool, then send `YOU ARE BEING TRANSFERRED TO A HUMAN AGENT. PLEASE HOLD ON.` — [main_policy.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/telecom/main_policy.md). Airline tasks: of tasks 0–31 and 33–49 inspected, only task 13 expects `transfer_to_human_agents` ("If the agent says that the change is not possible, you ask to be transferred") — [tasks.json](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/main/data/tau2/domains/airline/tasks.json).
- Follow-on: PolicyGuide (arXiv 2608.19861, 2026) targets policy-compliant workflows across airline/retail/telecom and notes telecom's user-side tools the agent cannot call — [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.19861). A τ³-bench (March 2026) with a banking domain and voice evaluation is mentioned by a secondary source and was not verified — [benchmarkingagents.com](https://benchmarkingagents.com/tau2-bench).

### Inferences
- The benchmark's most transferable lesson is that policy compliance is *inconsistent*, not absent: the same model follows the rule in one run and not another (pass^k decay). Any escalation rule that lives only in the prompt will inherit that variance; the brief's hard triggers belong in code.
- pass^k is a usable acceptance metric for the escalation eval: run each tagged message k=3–5 times and require all runs to agree on `escalate` for the hard-trigger class; report pass^k alongside P/R.
- The benchmarks treat escalation as rare (one task in ~50 airline tasks) and as a correct outcome only after the agent has refused within policy and the user insists — matching the brief's "no tool and no rule can do this" category.
- The "required substring" output check (e.g., "54.04") is a cheap pattern for the handoff sentence: assert the exact constant appears iff `escalate=true`.

### Gaps
- Neither paper reports per-task or per-category pass rates for transfer tasks ("none reported").
- The tau-bench failure-category percentages are partly garbled in the HTML rendering; the ~55% figure should be checked against the PDF.
- Task 32 in tau2 airline was only partially visible; the transfer-task count is "at least 1 of 50."

## Regulatory or industry guidance on disclosure and handoff for AI in healthcare-adjacent customer service (2024–2026)

### Takeaway
Three concrete rules apply or may apply: EU AI Act Article 50(1) (in force 2026-08-02) requires that people be told they are interacting with an AI unless obvious; Utah SB 226 (effective 2025-05-07) requires prominent disclosure for "high-risk" generative-AI interactions in regulated occupations, including those collecting health information or giving medical advice, with a safe harbor if the AI itself discloses at the outset and throughout; California AB 3030 (operative 2025) requires a disclaimer and instructions for reaching a human provider on AI-generated clinical communications from covered California providers, exempting provider-reviewed and non-clinical messages. None prescribe a handoff trigger; all push toward disclosure plus an obvious path to a human.

### Cited Findings
- EU AI Act Art. 50(1): "Providers shall ensure that AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons are designed" so that people are informed, unless "obvious from the point of view of a natural person who is reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect"; information "at the latest at the time of the first interaction"; Article 50 "Comes into force 2 August 2026, according to Article 113"; paragraphs 1–2 bind providers, 3–4 deployers — [artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/). Secondary sources agree customer-service chatbots are in scope and that the Digital Omnibus delayed only Art. 50(2) marking (to 2026-12-02 for pre-existing systems), not 50(1) — [secureprivacy.ai](https://secureprivacy.ai/blog/eu-ai-act-article-50-transparency-obligations-for-chatbots-and-deepfakes-2026), [jorijn.com](https://jorijn.com/en/blog/eu-ai-act-website-chatbot-disclosure-august-2026/) (vendor/practitioner blogs; fine amounts conflict between them).
- Utah SB 226 (signed 2025-03-27, effective 2025-05-07): a person in a regulated occupation shall "prominently disclose when an individual receiving services is interacting with generative artificial intelligence ... if the use ... constitutes a high-risk artificial intelligence interaction"; disclose "verbally at the start of a verbal interaction; and in writing before the start of a written interaction"; high-risk includes collecting health, financial, or biometric information and "Providing financial, legal, medical, or mental health advice or services"; safe harbor where the AI "clearly and conspicuously discloses it is nonhuman ... at the outset of the interaction and throughout" — [enrolled bill, le.utah.gov](https://le.utah.gov/Session/2025/bills/enrolled/SB0226.pdf), summarized by [Perkins Coie](https://perkinscoie.com/insights/update/new-utah-ai-laws-change-disclosure-requirements-and-identity-protections-target) and [FPF](https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Overview-of-Utahs-2025-Enacted-AI-Legislation.pdf).
- California AB 3030 (Health & Safety Code §1339.75): covered "health facilities, clinics, physicians groups and other health care providers" must disclose generative-AI patient communications; disclaimer "at the top of a letter or at the beginning of an audio message," and for chat "featured prominently throughout the communication"; exempt if the communication lacks "patient clinical information" or "if a health care provider reviewed the communication" — [Petrie-Flom Center, Harvard Law, 2024-10-17](http://petrieflom.law.harvard.edu/2024/10/17/health-care-ai-and-the-law-an-emerging-regulatory-landscape-in-california/). Law-firm summaries add that the message must tell patients how to reach a human provider and that non-clinical messages (appointment reminders, billing) are exempt — [Morgan Lewis, 2025-04](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/04/aint-done-yet-states-continue-to-craft-rules-to-manage-ai-tools-in-healthcare) (statute text at leginfo.legislature.ca.gov returned 403; not verified against primary text).
- Illinois SB 2259 and Indiana HB 1620 were proposed as AB 3030-style bills — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/04/aint-done-yet-states-continue-to-craft-rules-to-manage-ai-tools-in-healthcare).
- Utah HB 452 (2025) covers mental-health chatbots specifically — [Sheppard Mullin](https://www.sheppard.com/insights/blogs/utah-enacts-ai-amendments-targeted-at-mental-health-chatbots-and-generative-ai); not applicable to hair-transplant coordination.

### Inferences
- A medical-tourism coordinator texting patients about hair transplants is plausibly a "high-risk" interaction under Utah's definition (collects health information, may touch medical advice) if any patient is in Utah, and is in scope of EU Art. 50(1) for EU patients; the cheapest compliance is the Utah safe-harbor pattern: the bot identifies itself as an AI in the first SMS and keeps a standing "reply HUMAN to reach a coordinator" line.
- AB 3030 applies only if the company is a covered California provider and the SMS contains clinical information; scheduling and billing messages are exempt by most readings. Treat applicability as a legal question for counsel.
- None of these rules define escalation triggers, so the product-side designs above are not constrained by regulation beyond disclosure and an available human path.

### Gaps
- AB 3030 primary text not retrieved (403 on leginfo and Justia); the "instructions for reaching a human provider" element rests on law-firm summaries.
- No FTC or HHS guidance specific to AI handoff in customer service was found in this research.
